Eastsora Sells Two Heated Throws, Both Claiming 12 Heat Levels

Search "Eastsora heated throw" on Amazon and two listings come back from the same brand, both finished at 50×60″, both printing "12 Heat Levels" on the box, both carrying ETL and FCC marks. The thumbnails look almost interchangeable until you read past the headline spec: one is a ribbed flannel throw, the other is faux fur bonded over flannel, and that fabric choice is the only real decision left once the size and the level count are already settled.

Quick answer

Same brand, same size, same level count — the fabric is the decision

Eastsora's two current heated throws are both 50×60″ and both print "12 Heat Levels" on the listing, so the spec most shoppers check first doesn't separate them at all. The Soft Ribbed Faux Fur Flannel throw (ASIN B0FL7N1T23, $25.07) builds the heating wire into a flannel base with a faux fur face layered on top, which is the heavier, plusher of the two. The plain Flannel Blanket Throw (ASIN B0FL3811B3, $36.99) skips the fur layer and keeps a single ribbed flannel face. Both list the same twelve heat levels, the same 1–12H timer language, the same auto shut-off and overheat protection, and both state ETL and FCC marks on the listing. The faux fur throw is also the cheaper of the two, which is the opposite of what the thicker fabric might suggest.

What's identical

Same brand, same size, same twelve-level claim: what's left to differ

This is a pattern we keep running into on this site — a single brand listing more than one product that shares the exact same headline specs. We saw it with Westinghouse's three heated throws and with ESTINGO's four queen blankets, and Eastsora's catalogue follows the same shape: two listings, one brand, nearly identical spec sheets on paper.

Both Eastsora throws finish at the standard single-person throw size, 50×60″, the size that covers a lap on the sofa or drapes over the shoulders in a chair — neither is sold as an oversized or XL variant. Both list twelve heat levels and describe the timer the same way: "1–12H," language both listings use to mean you can select a run time from one to twelve hours rather than choosing from a short list of fixed presets. Both state auto shut-off and overheat protection as standard safety features, both are listed as machine washable, and both carry ETL and FCC marks printed directly in the listing title.

The price is where the two listings actually separate, and the gap runs the opposite direction from what the fabric might suggest: the faux fur throw (B0FL7N1T23) is $25.07, nearly $12 less than the plain ribbed flannel throw (B0FL3811B3) at $36.99. A thicker, two-layer fabric construction costing less than a single-layer one is not the ordering you'd guess from the fabric name alone, which is exactly why the fabric line in each listing is worth reading rather than assumed.

The fabric

Ribbed flannel versus faux fur over flannel: feel and heat retention

The $36.99 listing is named, plainly, Flannel Blanket Throw — a single ribbed flannel face over the heating wire, with no second fabric layer. Ribbed flannel is light, dries quickly after a wash, and is the most common fabric choice in this category across most brands, not just Eastsora's. Because it's a single layer, less of the blanket's warmth comes from the fabric itself; more of it comes from the heating element doing the work, which is part of why this style of throw responds quickly when you raise the heat level.

The $25.07 listing names its construction directly in the title too: Soft Ribbed Faux Fur Flannel. That's a ribbed flannel base with a faux fur face layered over it, which is a heavier, plusher build than a single flannel face. A pile fabric like faux fur traps air against the body the way any thick blanket does, independent of whether the heating element is switched on, so this throw is likely to feel warmer at a given heat level simply because the fabric itself is doing more of the retaining. That's a property of the fabric construction, not a claim about the heating wire running any differently between the two.

Neither listing publishes a fabric weight in grams, so there's no published number to compare directly — the distinction here is construction (single layer versus two bonded layers), not a gsm figure we can set side by side. What is published and comparable is the fabric name itself, and on both Eastsora listings that name sits right in the title, which makes it the first thing worth reading rather than the easiest thing to skip past on the way to the price.

Safety marks

ETL and FCC marks printed on both listings

Both Eastsora throws state ETL and FCC marks directly in the listing title, not buried in the bullet points. ETL is a third-party electrical-safety certification mark, functionally the counterpart to UL certification on this kind of product, and it's the mark worth looking for on anything with a heating element you plan to leave running unattended. FCC marks relate to the electromagnetic-interference standard the US requires for electronic devices generally; on a heated blanket it's a standard compliance line rather than a differentiator, since nearly every electronic consumer product sold in the US carries one.

What matters here is that neither Eastsora listing is stronger than the other on this point — both state the same two marks, so certification isn't a reason to prefer the flannel throw over the faux fur one or the reverse. Both also list auto shut-off and overheat protection as standard, which are the features that actually govern safe unattended use night after night, more than either mark does on its own. If you're comparing Eastsora's two throws specifically, certification is one more line where the two listings read identically — the fabric and the price remain the places where they actually differ.

A different brand's claim

Ten levels from another brand against twelve from Eastsora

To check whether Eastsora's twelve heat levels buy you anything in practice, we lined both throws up against the greenoak Heated Blanket Electric Throw (ASIN B0G9L59V2R, $26.99), a 50×60″ fleece-sherpa throw from a different brand that lists ten heat levels rather than twelve, and a different auto-off structure — four fixed timer options (1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 hours) rather than the continuous 1–12H range both Eastsora throws use.

Two extra steps between level 10 and level 12 on a dial you're adjusting by feel, usually in a dark room, is a difference that's easy to state and hard to actually perceive. Neither Eastsora listing nor the greenoak listing publishes a temperature range in degrees for each level, so there's no published figure that tells us how many degrees separate level 10 from level 12 on a twelve-level throw, or whether greenoak's ten levels span the same overall temperature range in coarser steps or a narrower one. Without that number from either brand, the honest answer is that "twelve levels" is a bigger number than "ten levels" on the box, and that's the only thing we can confirm from the published specs — not that the extra granularity is something you'd reliably notice with your hand on the dial.

Where the three throws do differ in a way you'd actually feel is the timer structure. Eastsora's continuous 1–12H range lets you dial in any whole hour you want; greenoak's four fixed presets (1/2/4/6/8 hours) mean picking the closest preset to what you actually want, which matters most in the middle of the range — wanting roughly five hours of run time means rounding down to four or up to six on the greenoak throw, while either Eastsora throw lets you set five directly. That's a real, usable difference. The level-count gap, on the published specs we have, is not one we can confirm the same way.

The decision

Which Eastsora throw to buy if the fabric choice is the only real decision

Once you've confirmed both Eastsora throws are 50×60″ with twelve heat levels, the same timer language, the same auto shut-off and overheat protection, and the same ETL and FCC marks, there's genuinely one spec left to decide between them: the fabric. Read the listing title, not just the thumbnail — "Soft Ribbed Faux Fur Flannel" versus "Flannel Blanket Throw" tells you everything the rest of the comparison can't, because the two fabrics photograph almost identically at thumbnail size on a white background.

If you want the plusher, heavier feel and you're comfortable with a longer dry time after washing, buy the faux fur throw (B0FL7N1T23) — it's also $11.92 cheaper than the plain flannel listing, so there's no price trade-off to weigh against the fabric preference. If you'd rather have a lighter blanket that dries faster and don't need the extra fur layer, buy the plain flannel throw (B0FL3811B3), knowing you're paying more for less fabric; the premium there appears to track inventory or listing positioning rather than any feature the flannel-only throw has that the faux fur one lacks.

Either way, don't let the twelve-level claim do the deciding for you against a ten-level throw from another brand. On the published specs available, the fabric and the timer structure are the differences you'd actually notice day to day; two extra heat levels on a dial, without a published temperature range behind them, is not one we can confirm you would.
