Before you buy

Four things that decide whether you'll be happy

01

Throw or bed size

A 50×60″ throw is one person on a sofa. A queen at 84×90″ covers a bed for two and costs roughly twice as much. Most people searching for a heated blanket picture the throw — decide which before you compare anything else.

02

One controller or two

On a shared bed, a single controller is the thing that turns into a nightly negotiation. Dual-zone models give each side its own settings. On a throw it is irrelevant, which is why only one pick here has it.

03

How long it stays on

Automatic shut-off is standard on US models, but the length is not. Some cut out after one or two hours, which is right for a sofa and wrong for a bed. If you want it running all night, the auto-off range is the specification to read first.

04

Whether it washes

Nearly all of these are machine washable — after you unplug the controller from the blanket. Check that the cord detaches, and never put the controller itself anywhere near water.

The part nobody shows you

What it actually costs to run

The arithmetic

Cents, not dollars — with the assumptions stated

Almost no heated-blanket listing publishes a wattage, so any running-cost figure you read is built on an assumption. Here is ours, in the open. Assume a throw draws about 100 watts on a mid setting and you run it for eight hours: that is 0.8 kWh. At roughly 17 cents per kilowatt-hour — near the US residential average — the evening costs about 14 cents. A full-size bed blanket draws more, so call it double. Change the wattage or your local rate and the number moves, but the order of magnitude does not: running one of these all evening costs less than heating an empty room for a few minutes. That is the entire case for the category, and it is why they sell every time energy prices are in the news.

Living with one

Wash it, but unplug the controller first

Every blanket here is machine washable once the controller is detached from the cord — that detachment is the thing to confirm in the listing before you buy, because it is what makes the difference between a washable blanket and a dry-clean one. Never submerge or wash the controller itself. Do not tuck a heated blanket tightly under a mattress or fold it while it is running, since a sharp crease concentrates heat on the wire. Automatic shut-off is standard on US-market models and the certifications to look for are ETL or UL; two of our picks state their specific testing programmes, which is more than most of the category does. If you have reduced sensation in your skin or any condition affecting circulation, treat the heat setting as something to discuss with a doctor — that is general information, not medical advice.

FAQ

Common questions

Can you leave a heated blanket on all night?

That depends on the blanket, and it is a specification rather than a matter of opinion. Every US-market model here shuts itself off automatically, but the interval varies: our top pick lets you choose anything from one hour to twelve, the Bedsure models run to ten hours, and some budget throws cut out after two. If you want warmth through the night, read the auto-off range before anything else — a blanket that stops after ninety minutes is a sofa blanket whatever the box says. Manufacturers design the shut-off deliberately, so overriding it is not something to look for.

Can you put a heated blanket in the washing machine?

Almost always yes, once you unplug the controller from the blanket and set it aside. The fabric and the heating wire are built to survive a gentle cycle; the electronics are not. Check the listing for a detachable cord before you buy — it is the single detail that decides whether a blanket is genuinely washable. Wash cold or warm on a gentle cycle, and dry on low or air-dry. Heavier sherpa and faux-fur throws take considerably longer to dry than flannel ones, which is worth knowing if you only own one.

How much electricity does a heated blanket use?

Very little, and that is the whole point of the category. Working from a stated assumption — about 100 watts for a throw, eight hours of use, and roughly 17 cents per kilowatt-hour — an evening comes to around 14 cents, with a full-size bed blanket costing roughly double. We show the arithmetic rather than a headline figure because manufacturers rarely publish wattage, so every number in this category rests on an assumption somebody made. Compared with raising the thermostat for a whole house, it is not close.

Should I get a throw or a full-size blanket?

Ask where you will use it. A 50×60″ throw is one person on a sofa or an armchair, folds into a cupboard, and costs $27 to $45. A queen at roughly 84×90″ covers a bed for two, costs $60 to $71, and is awkward on a sofa. If you are buying for the bed and you share it, the dual-zone question matters more than the size one. If you are unsure whether you will use a heated blanket at all, buy the cheap throw first — almost nobody regrets that order.

Are heated blankets safe?

Modern ones are built around the assumption that they will be left on: automatic shut-off is standard, and the marks to look for are ETL or UL certification, with overheat protection stated explicitly on some models. Two of our picks go further and name their testing programme — one cites an Intertek-recognised laboratory and seventy-four tests, another states seventy-eight independent safety tests. The practical rules are the boring ones: don't fold or tightly tuck a blanket while it is running, don't sleep on top of one that is meant to go over you, keep liquids away from the controller, and replace any blanket with a frayed cord or a scorched patch rather than repairing it.
