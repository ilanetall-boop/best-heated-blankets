The default, amplified

Why a heated throw skews even further toward grey than a queen blanket does

We've made this argument once already for queen-size blankets: grey is the lowest-return-risk default in home textiles, and heated blankets lean on that bias harder than most categories. A throw amplifies the effect rather than repeating it. It is the cheapest entry point into this category, it is bought more on impulse than a queen blanket is, and it typically lives somewhere more visible than a bedroom — a sofa, an armchair, a home office — which is exactly the kind of purchase a brand wants to make as close to risk-free as possible. Fewer dollars at stake per unit means less margin to fund a second photoshoot, a second listing, or a second production run in a color that might not sell.

We don't have to argue this in the abstract; our own recommendations make the point for us. Of the six blankets and throws in our main buying guide, three are throws and three are bed-size blankets. All three throws ship in grey. Of the three bed-size picks, only one — a $99.99 queen blanket — comes in a color that isn't some shade of grey. Put differently: every throw we recommend elsewhere on this site is grey, and the one pick that isn't grey at all is the most expensive thing in the guide, sized for a bed rather than a sofa. That is the pattern this article is built around, not a coincidence of which products happened to be good.

The mechanism is the same one we documented for queen blankets, just sharper. Amazon and Google both sort a search like "heated throw blanket" by relevance and sales volume, and both favor whichever color already sells the most. At queen size, at least a few brands invest in a second listing photographed properly in navy or cream. At throw size, the catalogue is dominated by low-cost, near-identical SKUs where a colorway often means nothing more than a swatch reachable by clicking through the gallery — the primary photo stays grey. Finding a throw genuinely photographed and specified in something other than grey took noticeably more digging than the same search at queen size.

Past the photo

What each of these three actually lists beyond the swatch

The HOMLYNS Heated Throw Blanket, Purple is the most thoroughly specified of the three. Its listing states five heat settings running from 86°F to 115°F, a three-hour auto shut-off, and ETL certification backed by what it describes as Japanese-imported heating wire with NTC and PTC sensors monitoring both spot and surface temperature. The fabric is flannel on one face and sherpa on the other — the same combination we cover on its own terms in our guide to reversible flannel-and-sherpa throws. What the listing does not state is a finished dimension beyond the word "Throw" itself, so we're not going to claim this one measures 50×60″ the way the other two do; if the exact footprint matters to you, confirm it on the live listing before buying.

The GOTCOZY Heated Blanket, Rose publishes the next-fullest spec sheet and, on paper, the most capable one: ten heating levels adjustable up to 45°C (113°F) through a built-in remote, and a nine-hour auto shut-off you set anywhere from one to nine hours rather than a single fixed cutoff. It's confirmed at 50×60″, the standard throw footprint.

The RAYVOICE Pink Heated Blanket Electric Throw is also 50×60″, and its listing leans almost entirely on the pink checkered pattern and gift-occasion marketing copy — birthdays, housewarmings, white elephant gifts — plus a claim of "over 85% heat coverage." What it does not state anywhere we could find is a heat-setting count or an auto-off window, the two specifications every other throw in this comparison leads with. We're not going to invent one for it. None of the three publishes a cord length either, which is worth knowing if that's the detail you care about most; our separate guide to heated blankets with the longest power cord covers the handful of models in this catalogue that do state one.

The control question

Heat levels and auto-off, compared across pink, rose and purple

Line the published numbers up and the GOTCOZY is the clear outlier in capability, not just color. Ten heat levels give you far more room between "barely on" and "too much" than HOMLYNS's five, and a selectable one-to-nine-hour window lets the same throw suit someone who wants twenty minutes on the sofa and someone who wants it running most of an evening. HOMLYNS's three-hour auto shut-off is fixed rather than selectable — useful for a short sit, less useful if you want it on longer without getting up to reset it, though the trade is a documented overheat-protection system with two separate temperature sensors, which neither of the other two states.

RAYVOICE is the unknown in this comparison, and we'd rather say that plainly than guess at a number. A heated throw without a published heat-setting count or auto-off window isn't necessarily a bad blanket — plenty of budget throws run a basic rocker switch with one or two levels and a short, non-adjustable timer — but we can't tell you which, because the listing doesn't say and we have no additional data on this specific product. Treat it as a sofa throw you keep an eye on rather than one you set and forget, until the listing (or the box itself, once it arrives) tells you otherwise.

The arithmetic

Price spread at this size, and whether color tracks with it

The spread across these three is $3.30 — from $29.99 for the purple HOMLYNS to $33.29 for the rose GOTCOZY, with the pink RAYVOICE at $31.62 in between. Compare that to queen size, where the equivalent three purple, navy and cream dual-control blankets we reviewed separately spread across a full $37. At throw size, buying a color other than grey costs next to nothing extra on its own.

Unlike that queen comparison, though, the small gap here roughly tracks what's actually published. GOTCOZY is both the most expensive of the three and the one with the most heat levels, the longest adjustable timer, and a remote control none of the others has. HOMLYNS is the cheapest and still backs its lower price with a certification and a dual-sensor safety system the pricier GOTCOZY doesn't state. RAYVOICE sits in the middle on price while publishing the least hard data of the three — the one case here where the number on the price tag doesn't obviously buy you anything you can point to in writing.

Three listings from three different brands is still a thin sample, and we're not claiming it proves color-driven pricing is dead at throw size. What we can say is that, this time, the extra few dollars bought a specific, stated feature rather than just a different color on the same unspecified hardware — the opposite of what we found at queen size.

The other option

When grey is still the safer gift

Heated throws are bought as gifts more often than most items in this category, which is exactly the situation where color stops being a neutral choice. If you know the room the throw is going into and you know the person likes pink, rose or purple, one of these three is a fine, specific gift. If you don't know either of those things — a coworker, a distant relative, someone whose living room you've never seen — grey is the lower-risk default for the same reason manufacturers lean on it: it fits almost any room it lands in, and nobody has ever been upset to receive a blanket in a color that simply didn't clash with anything.

Grey also tends to come with more information attached, for the same reason it dominates search results: it's usually the primary listing a brand markets hardest, so it's more likely to state a full heat-setting count, an auto-off window, and a certification than a secondary colorway does — exactly the gap we found with RAYVOICE above. For the fuller-spec end of this category, see our guide to whether an XL heated throw is worth the upgrade. And if the gift is really for someone's bed rather than their sofa, our best queen heated blankets under $60 covers that budget question at bed size.

The short version: buy the color when you know it's right for the room and the person. Buy grey when you're not sure, because grey is the one choice in this category that has never once been the wrong one.

FAQ

Common questions

Why are heated throws even more likely to be grey than queen blankets?

A throw is the cheapest, most impulse-bought entry point into this category, and it's typically used somewhere more visible — a sofa or a living room — than a bedroom. That combination gives manufacturers less margin to fund a second color's photography and listing, and a stronger reason to default to the color least likely to clash. Every throw in our own main buying guide ships in grey; the only non-grey pick among our six recommendations is a $99.99 queen blanket, not a throw.

Do these three pink, rose and purple throws all have the same heat settings?

No. GOTCOZY (rose) publishes ten heat levels up to 45°C with a one-to-nine-hour adjustable timer. HOMLYNS (purple) publishes five settings from 86°F to 115°F with a fixed three-hour auto shut-off. RAYVOICE (pink) does not publish a heat-setting count or an auto-off window at all in its listing, and we have no additional data to fill that gap.

Does a colored heated throw cost more than a grey one?

At this size, barely. The three throws in this guide span just $3.30, from $29.99 to $33.29 — far narrower than the $37 spread we found among colored, dual-control queen blankets. Here, the small gap roughly tracks what's actually published: the most expensive of the three also has the most heat levels and the longest timer, unlike at queen size, where the priciest option didn't publish anything the cheaper ones didn't.

Is a colored heated throw a safe gift if I don't know someone's taste?

It's a reasonable gift if you know the room or the person's color preference; otherwise grey is the lower-risk choice, for the same reason manufacturers default to it — it fits almost any room and rarely clashes with existing decor. Grey models also tend to carry fuller published specifications, since they're usually the listing a brand markets hardest.
