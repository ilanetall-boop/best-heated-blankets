Westinghouse Sells Three Heated Throws: Here's What Actually Differs

Search "Westinghouse heated throw" on Amazon and three separate listings come back, from the same brand, in the same 50×60″ throw size, with the same six heat levels advertised on the box. The prices sit close together too — $35.69, $39.99, and $39.99. Spend five minutes with the actual listing copy instead of the thumbnail and the differences stop being cosmetic: fabric, a memory function, timer behavior, and one wash-care instruction that matters more than any of it.

By Ilane Tall · Updated November 12, 2026 · Research-based.
Quick answer

Same brand, same size, same heat levels — not the same blanket

Westinghouse's three current heated throws (ASINs B09B925MYF, B08K37WQ76, and B09F6FTL42) are all 50×60″ and all run six heat levels, so the spec sheet most shoppers skim to first tells you almost nothing. What actually separates them is the fabric on your skin, whether the controller remembers your last setting, how the auto-off timer is set, and whether you can machine-wash it or have to hand it cold-water-only. The cheapest of the three, the plain-flannel Soft Flannel throw at $35.69, is also the simplest to operate. The two at $39.99 split the rest of the differences between them: one is a reversible flannel-sherpa throw with a memory-heat controller and a cold-wash-only care label, the other is a 240g chenille throw that's ETL certified and machine washable.

Below we go through each difference in the order it actually affects you — fabric first, then the controller, then the timer, then the one wash-care line that changes how you live with it — before landing on which of the three to buy and what to check if the listing photos all look the same to you.

What's identical

Three listings, one brand, three nearly-matching spec sheets

This isn't the first time we've run into a single brand's catalogue holding multiple listings that look, at a glance, like the same product priced differently — we found the same pattern when we lined up FVERWAG's three queen-size listings. Westinghouse's version is smaller in scale but the question is identical: when three SKUs from one brand share the headline specs, where does the money actually go?

Here, the headline specs really are shared. All three throws finish at 50×60″, the standard single-person throw size that covers a lap on the sofa or drapes over shoulders in a chair — none of the three is sold as an XL or oversized variant. All three list six heat levels, not four and not ten, so the control granularity is identical no matter which one you buy. And all three carry the Westinghouse name and the charcoal-or-grey color palette the brand tends to default to for this category.

The prices track close together but aren't identical: $35.69 for the Soft Flannel throw (ASIN B09B925MYF), and $39.99 apiece for the Electric Blanket Heated Throw in grey (B08K37WQ76) and the ETL Certified throw (B09F6FTL42). A $4.30 spread across three otherwise-matching listings is a small enough gap that most shoppers would reasonably assume it's rounding, or stock-level pricing, rather than a real product difference. It isn't rounding. It tracks fabric, one controller feature, and a wash-care instruction that genuinely changes how often you can throw the blanket in the machine.

The fabric

Plain flannel, reversible flannel-sherpa, or 240g chenille

The $35.69 throw is named, in the listing itself, Soft Flannel — a single-sided flannel face over the heating wire, the simplest and most common fabric choice in this category and the reason it's also the cheapest of the three. Flannel is lightweight, dries quickly after a wash, and doesn't trap as much heat against the body as a pile fabric would, which is part of why the plain-flannel version tends to sit at the entry price point across most brands, not just Westinghouse.

The grey $39.99 listing (B08K37WQ76) is built as a reversible throw: flannel on one face, sherpa pile on the other, bonded to the same heating wire so flipping the blanket changes the texture against your skin without touching the heat setting underneath. We've written about this construction in more depth in our guide to reversible flannel-sherpa throws — the short version is that the second side is a texture and warmth-retention choice, not a second heating zone, and it's the reason this listing costs more than the plain-flannel one at the same size and heat-level count.

The ETL Certified $39.99 listing (B09F6FTL42) uses a different fabric again: 240g chenille, a denser, looped-pile fabric than flannel that reads heavier in the hand and holds its shape through repeated washing better than a thin flannel face does. Chenille sits in a similar price bracket to a reversible flannel-sherpa throw industry-wide, which is consistent with this listing landing at the same $39.99 as the grey reversible one rather than closer to the $35.69 flannel throw.

None of this shows up clearly in a product photo. A folded throw on a white background looks soft in every thumbnail regardless of which of the three fabrics it actually is — chenille's pile and sherpa's pile photograph almost identically at thumbnail size, and plain flannel photographs like any other blanket. The fabric name in the listing title or bullet points is the only reliable way to tell these three textures apart before the box arrives.

The controller

Memory heating, and what it changes day to day

Of the three, only the grey $39.99 reversible throw (B08K37WQ76) — the middle listing of the three in both price and position in Westinghouse's lineup — is built with a memory-heat controller. In practice, that means the blanket remembers whatever heat level you last used and resumes there the next time you turn it on, instead of resetting to level one or to off and making you click back up through the levels every single evening.

It's a small feature on paper and a genuinely noticeable one in use, specifically because heated throws get turned on and off repeatedly across a single evening — warming up, then switching off once you're warm, then back on twenty minutes later. A controller without memory means re-selecting your usual setting every time; one with it means pressing on and getting straight back to where you left off. The other two Westinghouse throws don't list this feature, so on the plain-flannel and the chenille throw, expect to reset the level each time you power the blanket back on.

This is also a case where the fine print on the controller is doing more of the differentiating work than the fabric is. A shopper comparing three product photos has no way to see a memory function; it only shows up in the bullet copy, usually several lines down from the fabric and size claims that get top billing on the listing.

Auto-off

Two timers that slide, one that clicks between four presets

The grey reversible throw (B08K37WQ76) and the ETL Certified chenille throw (B09F6FTL42) both list a continuous auto-off timer running from 2 to 10 hours — you dial in roughly how long you want the blanket to stay on within that range, rather than choosing from a short fixed list. The $35.69 Soft Flannel throw (B09B925MYF) instead lists four fixed timer presets: 2, 4, 8, and 10 hours. You pick one of those four durations; there's no setting in between.

For most evening use on the sofa, this distinction barely matters — the real-world difference between an auto-off set to 2 hours continuous and an auto-off set to the 2-hour preset is nothing at all, they're the same two hours. Where it starts to matter is the middle of the range: if you want the blanket to run for roughly six hours, the continuous-timer throws let you set six directly, while the preset throw forces a choice between the 4-hour preset (shorter than you wanted) and the 8-hour preset (longer than you wanted). It's a minor convenience gap, not a safety difference — all three throws do have an auto-off, which is the feature that actually matters for unattended use.

Care instructions

The one line that changes how often you'll actually wash it

This is the difference most likely to be buried past where a shopper stops reading. The grey reversible flannel-sherpa throw (B08K37WQ76) — the same listing that carries memory heating and the 240g-adjacent price point — is specified as cold-wash-only. That isn't the same thing as "not machine washable"; it almost certainly can still go in a home washer. It means skipping a warm or hot cycle, which matters for a reversible fabric bonded to a heating wire, because heat and agitation together are harder on a bonded two-fabric construction than on a single flannel face.

The plain-flannel Soft Flannel throw (B09B925MYF) and the ETL Certified chenille throw (B09F6FTL42) are both listed as standard machine washable, with no cold-only restriction stated. In practical terms, that means less thought required at laundry time: toss it in with a normal load rather than remembering to switch the water temperature down specifically for one blanket.

On every heated blanket, regardless of which of these three you buy, the controller itself has to be unclipped and kept out of the wash entirely — that's standard across the category and true of all three Westinghouse throws, not a difference between them. The cold-wash instruction is the one real differentiator, and it's worth weighing against the memory-heat feature on the same listing: you're trading a slightly more careful laundry routine for a controller that remembers your setting and a reversible second texture. Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on how often you expect to wash the thing.

If you can't tell them apart

Which one to buy when the listing photos all look identical

Open all three Westinghouse listings side by side on a phone and the product photos genuinely don't help — a folded charcoal or grey throw on a white background looks like the same blanket shot three times, because at thumbnail size, flannel, sherpa, and chenille pile all read as "soft fabric, neutral color." The things that actually separate these three live in the text, not the images, and they sit in a specific order of importance once you know what to look for.

Check the fabric name first — "flannel," "reversible," or "chenille" in the title or first bullet point tells you more about day-to-day feel than anything else on the page. Check the wash-care line second, specifically whether it says cold-wash-only; that single word changes your laundry routine more than any other spec here. Check for "memory" in the controller description third, since that's the feature most likely to go unmentioned in a scan of the bullet points even though it changes how the blanket behaves every single time you turn it on. The size and heat-level count, which is usually what's bolded at the top of the listing, is the one thing you can skip checking across these three — it's identical on all of them.

If none of that differentiation matters to you and you just want the cheapest functional six-level throw at this size, buy the Soft Flannel listing and stop there. If you expect to use it heavily enough that the memory function earns its keep, or you specifically want a reversible fabric, the grey listing is worth the cold-wash trade-off. If you want the heavier chenille feel and a certification mark with no laundry restriction, the ETL Certified listing is the one built for that.

FAQ

Common questions

Are Westinghouse's three heated throws actually different products?

Yes. All three share the same 50×60″ size and six heat levels, but they differ in fabric (plain flannel, reversible flannel-sherpa, or 240g chenille), in whether the controller has a memory-heat function, in timer behavior (a continuous 2–10 hour range versus four fixed presets), and in wash care (cold-wash-only versus standard machine washable). The $4.30 price spread tracks those differences rather than being arbitrary.

What does "memory heating" mean on a heated throw?

A memory-heat controller remembers the last heat level you used and resumes at that level the next time you switch the blanket on, rather than resetting to level one or to off. It saves you from re-selecting your usual setting every time you turn the throw back on, which matters because heated throws tend to get switched on and off repeatedly across an evening rather than left running continuously.

Does "cold-wash-only" mean I can't machine wash it?

No — it almost certainly means the blanket can still go in a home washing machine, just on a cold-water cycle rather than warm or hot. On the Westinghouse reversible flannel-sherpa throw, that instruction exists because heat and agitation together are harder on a bonded two-fabric construction than on a single flannel face. Always unclip the controller before washing any heated blanket, regardless of water temperature.

Which Westinghouse heated throw should I buy?

If price is the deciding factor, the $35.69 Soft Flannel throw gives you the same size and heat-level count as the other two for less money, with a simpler fixed-preset timer and no memory function. If you want a reversible fabric and a controller that remembers your setting, the $39.99 grey throw is worth its cold-wash-only care instruction. If you want a heavier chenille fabric, ETL certification, and no wash-temperature restriction, the $39.99 ETL Certified throw is the better fit.
