Sunbeam's Two Queen Blankets: Fleece or Microplush, and What Changes

Sunbeam sells the identical queen size twice. Same company, same 90×84″ bed, same basic controller — just a different fabric name stamped on the listing and a different number on the price tag. Those two products are also the only ones in our catalogue that name a fabric at all; a third queen from an unbranded import seller doesn't name one on anything. What that naming actually buys you, in heat settings and dollars rather than marketing language, is the question this guide answers.

By Ilane Tall · Updated November 9, 2026 · Research-based.
Quick answer

The electronics are identical; the fabric and the price are not

Sunbeam's Royal Ultra Fleece ($91.07) and Royal Luxe Microplush ($79.74) are the same queen blanket wearing two different outer shells. Both publish twelve heat settings, a digital controller with a backlit display, and a selectable auto-off capped at twelve hours. The difference you're actually paying for is the pile — fleece's brushed, visible texture against microplush's smoother, denser knit — plus whichever color each happens to come in. A third queen in the catalogue, an unbranded 84×90″ blanket from Molatink at $53.19, skips naming a fabric and also skips several things both Sunbeams include: a stated backlit display, more than five heat levels, and a timer you can set rather than one fixed at ten hours.

This guide walks through why Sunbeam duplicates the listing at all, what "fleece" and "microplush" actually promise as fabrics, the real size of the price gap between Sunbeam's own two, what the cheaper generic quietly leaves out, our picks among the three, and whether naming a fabric is worth paying for in the first place. The duplication

Why Sunbeam sells the same queen size in two fabrics

Look past the names and the two Sunbeam queens are the same product. Both are listed at queen dimensions, both run on a digital controller with an LED-backlit display meant to be readable at night, both offer twelve heat settings, and both cap their auto-off at twelve hours with the option to choose a shorter window. The microplush listing adds the words "overheat protection" explicitly; the fleece listing describes the same safety idea as "heat-sensing technology" that helps hold a consistent temperature. Functionally, these are two names for the same engineering choice, not two different safety tiers.

What actually differs is cosmetic: fleece in Indigo Blue, microplush in a beige tone Sunbeam calls Mushroom, under two separate line names — Royal Ultra for the fleece, Royal Luxe for the microplush. Running two SKUs that share a controller and a heat-setting count but differ in pile and shade lets Sunbeam sell to two different bedroom aesthetics without building two different electronics packages. A shopper who wants a textured, visibly fuzzy throw-like finish picks fleece; a shopper who wants a smoother, almost suede-like surface picks microplush. Either way, Sunbeam only had to engineer the control system once.

It's also not a coincidence that this duplication shows up specifically at queen size. Queen is the default size for a shared adult bed, which makes it the size most buyers are actually shopping for when they land on a Sunbeam heated-blanket listing. Doubling the fabric options at the one size that gets the most traffic is a reasonable use of catalogue space; we don't see Sunbeam repeat the same trick at twin or king in this data set, which tracks with queen being the volume size worth splitting into two finishes rather than one.

The fabric

Fleece versus microplush: what the name actually promises

Fleece is a brushed synthetic pile that has been the default heated-blanket fabric for decades — a visibly textured, mid-loft surface that's lightweight relative to how warm it feels and dries quickly after a wash. Microplush is a finer, denser knit: shorter fibers packed more tightly, which trades fleece's visible nap for a smoother, almost suede-like hand-feel. Both are synthetic blends engineered to sit directly over a heating element without insulating so heavily that the wire's warmth can't reach you, which is also why heated blankets almost never use thick natural wool or real fur as the outer layer.

Here's the honest gap in what either name promises: neither Sunbeam listing publishes a temperature range, an R-value, or any other figure that would let you compare heat retention between the two fabrics directly. Both share the identical heat-setting count and the identical auto-off window, which means whatever warmth difference exists between fleece and microplush on this specific blanket isn't documented anywhere in the product data — it isn't a number either listing is willing to put in writing. What the name promises, based on what's actually published, is texture and finish, not thermal performance.

That makes this mostly a feel preference rather than a performance decision. Fleece tends to read as cozier and more visually "blanket-like," the kind of texture you'd expect folded over a sofa arm. Microplush tends to read as smoother and more polished, closer to bedding you'd find in a hotel room. If you've already read our comparison of flannel, sherpa, and faux fur fabrics, this is the same category of decision one size up the price ladder: the fabric name tells you what it will feel like against your skin, not how warm it will run.

The arithmetic

The gap between Sunbeam's own two queens

As of our most recent price checks, the microplush Royal Luxe runs $79.74 and the fleece Royal Ultra runs $91.07 — a gap of $11.33 on the days we looked. Amazon's Sunbeam pricing moves by the week rather than sitting still, and we've watched this specific spread drift toward fifteen dollars and narrow back toward ten depending on when either listing last repriced. Treat the exact figure as a snapshot, not a fixed fact, and check both live prices before assuming one is reliably cheaper than the other.

What matters more than the exact dollar figure is what that gap is and isn't buying. Both blankets publish twelve heat settings. Both publish a backlit digital controller. Both publish a selectable auto-off reaching twelve hours. None of that changes between the two listings — the premium on the fleece buys you fleece instead of microplush, a different color, and whatever cachet comes from "Royal Ultra" sounding a step above "Royal Luxe," rather than any additional functionality you could point to in the spec sheet. If you've read our piece on why the same FVERWAG blanket shows up under three listings, this is a cleaner version of the same phenomenon: a single company selling functionally equivalent products at different prices, with the differentiator living entirely outside the electronics.

The cheaper option

What the $53 generic leaves out that neither Sunbeam listing does

The third queen in this comparison, a Molatink-brand 84×90″ blanket at $53.19, doesn't name a single fabric — it names two, reversible flannel on one face and sherpa fleece on the other, which is actually a point in its favor: you get two textures from one purchase, and the fabric is explicitly reversible rather than fixed to a single feel. It also offers genuine dual-zone control, letting each side of the bed run its own temperature, which is a real feature neither single-sleeper Sunbeam queen includes at all.

But set the reversible fabric and the dual control aside, and the Molatink listing is quieter than either Sunbeam on three specific points. It publishes five heat settings against Sunbeam's twelve on both listings — less than half the granularity between "barely on" and "too much." Its timer is described as a fixed ten-hour auto-shutoff rather than a window you select yourself, so you can't dial it down to a two-hour nap setting the way both Sunbeam controllers allow. And its listing never mentions a backlit or digital display, the specific detail both Sunbeam blankets lead with for reading settings in a dark room; the Molatink's control panel may well have one, but if it does, the listing doesn't say so, and we don't assert specs that aren't published.

The same pattern holds on safety language. Sunbeam's microplush listing states overheat protection outright; the fleece listing describes heat-sensing technology aimed at the same goal. Molatink's listing frames its ten-hour timer as the safety feature — "no more worrying about forgetting to turn off your electric blanket" — but doesn't make a separate overheat-protection claim the way either Sunbeam does. None of this makes the generic a bad blanket; reversible fabric and real dual-zone control for $53.19 is a genuine value proposition we cover on its own terms in our queen blankets under $60 roundup. It just means the gap between "names a fabric" and "doesn't" lines up with a real gap in heat-setting count and timer flexibility, not just marketing language.

The verdict

Is naming a fabric worth the premium at all?

Split this into the two purchases it actually is, because they're not the same decision. The first jump, from the unnamed Molatink queen to either Sunbeam, buys a documented bundle: seven more heat settings, a timer you can set yourself instead of one fixed at ten hours, and a backlit display Sunbeam calls out specifically for night-time use. That bundle is worth paying for if finer heat control and a readable controller in the dark matter to how you'll actually use the blanket — it's real function, not branding, even though it happens to arrive wrapped in a named fabric.

The second jump, from microplush to fleece within Sunbeam's own lineup, is a different kind of purchase entirely. At roughly eleven dollars more and with every published spec identical, you are paying only for a preference — brushed, textured fleece instead of a smoother microplush knit, plus Indigo Blue instead of beige. There's nothing wrong with paying for that; a fabric you'll touch every night for a whole winter is a reasonable thing to have an opinion about. Just don't expect the fifteen-to-eleven-dollar gap to be buying you a warmer blanket, because neither listing claims it is, and the shared heat-setting count and timer window say it isn't.

The one thing none of this money buys, at any price point among these three, is independent temperature control for two sleepers — that's the Molatink's exclusive feature here, not Sunbeam's. If a shared bed with two different temperature preferences is the actual problem you're solving, the cheapest blanket in this comparison is the only one of the three built to solve it, which is worth weighing against how much you care about a dozen heat settings or a particular fabric name.

Common questions

What's actually different between Sunbeam's fleece and microplush queen blankets?

Mostly the fabric and the price. Both publish twelve heat settings, a backlit digital controller, and a selectable auto-off reaching twelve hours — the electronics are the same. The fleece version (Indigo Blue, $91.07) has a brushed, visibly textured pile; the microplush version (a beige Sunbeam calls Mushroom, $79.74) has a smoother, denser knit. The roughly eleven-dollar gap between them, which has run closer to fifteen dollars at times, buys the fabric and the color, not extra function.

Does fleece or microplush hold heat better?

Neither Sunbeam listing publishes a temperature range or any other figure that would answer that directly, and both share the identical heat-setting count and auto-off window. Based on what's actually documented, the difference between the two fabrics is tactile — texture and finish — not a measured difference in warmth retention. Treat any claim that one runs warmer than the other as a feel-based impression rather than a published spec.

Is the $53 generic queen blanket as good as a Sunbeam?

It depends on what you need. The Molatink queen offers genuine dual-zone control for couples and reversible flannel-and-sherpa fabric, which neither Sunbeam queen includes. But it publishes five heat settings against Sunbeam's twelve, a fixed ten-hour timer instead of a selectable window, and no stated backlit display. If independent temperature control matters more to you than heat-setting granularity, the generic is arguably the better buy, not the worse one.

Should I pay extra to get a named fabric?

The first step up, from the unnamed generic to either Sunbeam, buys a documented bundle of more heat settings, a selectable timer, and a backlit display — that's worth paying for if those features matter to your routine. The further step from microplush to fleece within Sunbeam's own lineup buys fabric feel and color only, since every other spec is identical between the two. Pay for that second step only if you have an actual preference for fleece's texture over microplush's.
