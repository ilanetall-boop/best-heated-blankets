Heated Throw or Heated Mattress Pad: Which One

Both plug in. Both get warm. That is roughly where the similarity ends. One sits on top of you and travels wherever you take it; the other lives under your fitted sheet and heats the mattress itself, in exactly one place, before you ever lie down. The right one depends on where you get cold — not on which plug-in bedding has the most settings.

By Ilane Tall · Updated October 10, 2026 · Research-based.
Quick answer

Over you, or under you

A heated throw or electric blanket sits on top of you and warms the air trapped against your body — it works on a sofa, in a chair, or spread over a bed, and the controller sits in your hand or clipped to the edge of the blanket. A heated mattress pad goes under your fitted sheet and heats the mattress surface itself, which means it only does one job, in one place: pre-warming a specific bed before you get into it. If you want portable warmth, buy a throw. If you want a bed that is already warm when you climb in, buy a pad.

The construction

Over you or under you: the physical difference

A heated throw or electric blanket is a soft textile layer — flannel, sherpa, or fleece — with a thin heating wire run through the weave in a grid pattern. You drape it over yourself the way you would any blanket, and the heat comes from two sources at once: the wire itself, and your own body heat, trapped against your skin by the fabric on top of you. That is why a flannel-and-sherpa throw like the LaffyLo Electric Heated Blanket (72×84″, full size) feels warm even before you plug it in — the sherpa pile alone holds heat, and the element just tops it up.

A heated mattress pad works the opposite way: a fitted, elastic-cornered pad that goes between the bare mattress and the fitted sheet, with the heating element spread across most of its surface rather than concentrated where you happen to be sitting. It heats the mattress top from below, so you are warmed by contact with a pre-heated surface rather than by a layer placed over you. Nothing in our research set is a mattress pad — all three products below are over-body throws and blankets — but this construction difference is the whole reason the two categories exist side by side rather than one replacing the other.

Position changes how efficiently the heat is used. A throw loses warmth upward into open room air the moment you lift an edge, which is why most throws run multiple heat settings — the LaffyLo offers ten, the Sunbeam queen blanket offers twelve — so you can compensate as airflow keeps changing. A mattress pad has almost no airflow to fight: it is pressed between your body weight, the sheet, and the mattress foam, so the heat has nowhere to escape except into you.

Controller placement follows the same logic. A throw's controller stays reachable while you use the blanket, clipped to the fabric edge or resting on the cushion beside you. A mattress pad's controller is set once, before bed, usually clipped to the nightstand, because once the sheet and comforter are pulled up over it you are not meant to reach underneath to adjust a dial.

Placement

Where each one actually gets used

A heated throw's whole appeal is that it is not fixed to one room. A 50×60″ throw moves from the sofa to the armchair to the back seat of a car on a cold commute, and folds into a cupboard in spring. A full-size or queen electric blanket, like the LaffyLo at 72×84″ or the Sunbeam at 90×84″, is portable in principle — it is just a larger blanket — but in practice it tends to stay on one bed, because repositioning something that size for a sofa nap is more effort than most people bother with.

A heated mattress pad never leaves the bed it was fitted to. It is secured under the sheet with elastic corner straps sized to a specific mattress dimension — twin, full, queen, or king — and the whole point of fitting it is that it stays put for the season. If your cold spots are a reading chair, a home office, or a couch during a movie, a throw answers that directly and a mattress pad cannot, because a mattress pad only exists on a bed.

The two also solve different moments of the day. A throw is an active-use product: you reach for it when you are already cold and sitting still, and the warmth stops mattering the second you get up. A mattress pad is a pre-use product — its job is finished, in the best case, before you even get into bed, and many people switch it off once they are under the covers and generating their own heat. That difference in timing matters more to which one suits a household than any single spec on the box.

Upkeep

Washing frequency, and why it differs

All three products we cover here are stated as machine washable, standard for this category once you unplug the controller — the fabric and heating wire are built for a gentle cycle, the electronics are not. A throw gets washed on roughly the same schedule as any other blanket in direct contact with your skin or your couch: whenever it visibly needs it, which in a household that uses one most evenings can mean every few weeks. It is also a manageable size for a standard washing machine — a 50×60″ throw or even a 72×84″ full-size blanket like the LaffyLo fits a normal residential drum without crowding it.

A heated mattress pad is washed far less often, and that is a function of where it sits rather than how dirty it gets. Shielded under a fitted sheet, it never faces the direct contact a throw gets. Most households wash a mattress pad at the start and end of the heating season, not weekly. The bigger issue is bulk: a queen or king mattress pad is considerably larger than a throw, and with its wiring and quilted layers it can be too bulky for a standard home washer and dryer, which is why some owners end up at a laundromat for this one item.

The practical rule is the same for both: confirm the controller detaches before you buy, since that single detail decides whether a blanket is washable at all. Never submerge or machine-wash the controller itself.

The arithmetic

Running cost and coverage

What it costs to run

Cents, not dollars — with the assumptions stated

Almost no listing in this category publishes a wattage, so any running-cost number, including ours, rests on a stated assumption. Assume a throw draws roughly 100 watts on a mid setting and runs for eight hours: that is 0.8 kWh. At about 17 cents per kilowatt-hour — near the US residential average — an evening on the LaffyLo or the Bedsure throw costs around 14 cents. A full-size or queen blanket like the LaffyLo or the Sunbeam draws more to cover a larger area, so call it roughly double. A mattress pad is typically used for a shorter, more concentrated pre-warm period rather than run all night, so its total energy use over an evening can land in a similar range even though its heated surface is larger — change the wattage or run time and the figure moves, but the order of magnitude does not.

What each one covers

Coverage is the real trade-off

A 50×60″ throw covers one person on a sofa and nothing more. A full-size electric blanket at 72×84″, like the LaffyLo, stretches to cover one adult generously in bed or two people tightly. A queen blanket at 90×84″, like the Sunbeam, is sized to cover two adults without either dragging it off the other. A heated mattress pad, by contrast, is sized to match the mattress exactly and heats the entire sleeping surface evenly rather than whatever happens to be under the blanket at a given moment — the main reason some people prefer a pad once they have tried both.

The other option

When an under-body pad is the right answer instead

None of the three picks above are mattress pads, and for some households that is the wrong category entirely. A heated mattress pad is the better buy when the problem you are solving is a cold mattress rather than a cold person sitting in a chair. If the sheets feel cold against your legs for the first several minutes every single night, a pad that pre-warms the whole sleeping surface solves that directly, in a way no over-body blanket can, since a blanket only warms the parts of you it is touching.

A pad is also the better choice for households that share a bed and want independent temperature control without buying a dual-zone blanket. Mattress pads sized for queen and king beds commonly split into two zones, one per sleeper, each with its own controller — the same idea as a dual-zone electric blanket, applied under the sheet instead of across the top.

The trade-offs run the other way. A pad is fitted to one specific bed, so it offers none of the portability that makes a throw useful on the sofa or in a reading chair. It is also typically switched off once you're under the covers rather than run all night at a steady setting, since the heat has nowhere to go once trapped under a comforter. And because it is larger and more heavily quilted, washing it is a bigger undertaking — once or twice a season rather than every few weeks, and a queen or king pad may need a larger machine than a standard home washer.

If your cold spots are a specific bed and nothing else, a mattress pad is worth researching on its own terms. If your cold spots move around the house — the couch, a home office chair, a reading nook — a throw or electric blanket like the three above answers that directly, and a pad cannot, because a pad only ever exists on one bed.

FAQ

Common questions

What's the real difference between a heated throw and a heated mattress pad?

A heated throw or electric blanket sits on top of you and warms you directly through a fabric layer with a heating wire woven through it. A heated mattress pad sits under your fitted sheet and warms the mattress surface itself, pre-heating the bed before you get in rather than warming your body while you sit still under it. One is portable and used wherever you are; the other is fitted to one bed and only does its job there.

Can I use a heated throw and a heated mattress pad together?

They are not designed to be layered directly on top of each other while both are running, and stacking two heating elements against each other is not something any manufacturer's instructions account for. In practice, most households use one or the other depending on the room: a mattress pad on the bed, and a throw like the LaffyLo, Bedsure, or Sunbeam for the sofa or a chair elsewhere in the house.

Which one is cheaper to run?

They land in a similar range once you account for how each is actually used. A throw run for eight hours at roughly 100 watts costs around 14 cents at a typical US electricity rate, with a full-size or queen blanket costing roughly double for the same run time because it covers more area. A mattress pad often runs for a shorter, more concentrated pre-warm period rather than all night, so its total energy use over an evening can come out similar to a throw's even though its heated surface is larger. Neither is expensive compared with heating a whole room.

How often do I need to wash each one?

A throw gets washed on the same rough schedule as any blanket in regular use — every few weeks in a household that reaches for it most evenings — and a 50×60″ or even a 72×84″ full-size blanket fits a standard home washer without trouble. A mattress pad is washed far less often, typically once or twice a season, because it sits under the sheet and never touches skin or spills directly; the trade-off is that a queen or king pad can be too bulky for some home washers and dryers. On both, unplug the controller before washing and never submerge it.
