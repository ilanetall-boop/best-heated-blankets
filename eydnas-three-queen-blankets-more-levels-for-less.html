Eydna Sells Three Queen Blankets, and the Cheapest Two Have More Heat Levels

Search "Eydna queen heated blanket" and three listings come back from the same brand, all finished at the same 84×90″ queen size, all running dual control. Two of them — light gray and white — cost $49.99 and list nine heating levels apiece. The third, denim blue, costs ten dollars more and lists five. That isn't a typo on either listing. It's the one number in this small catalogue that runs backwards from price, and it's worth understanding before you click any of the three.

By Ilane Tall · Updated November 24, 2026 · Research-based.
Quick answer

Nine levels for $49.99, five levels for $59.99

Eydna's light-gray microplush queen blanket (B0FFB37B3Y) and its white double-sided sherpa queen blanket (B0FFB8VXMY) are both priced at $49.99, both run dual controllers with independently heated zones, and both list nine heating levels with a selectable one-to-ten-hour auto-off. The denim-blue version (B0FC33PKHJ) costs $59.99 — ten dollars more — and lists only five heating levels, with its auto-off stated as a flat ten hours rather than a selectable range. Paying more for less control is the headline oddity here, and nothing in any of the three titles explains why.

Below we go through what's genuinely shared across all three, why the heat-level count runs opposite to price, what actually separates the two cheaper listings from each other, our picks among the three, and whether the denim-blue listing's one stated advantage — "Energy Efficient" — is worth the trade. What's identical

Same brand, same queen size, mostly the same control system

This is a familiar shape of question on this site — a single brand running several near-identical listings at the same size, and the shopper's job is figuring out which spec actually moves between them. We ran the same exercise against four ESTINGO queen blankets in our ESTINGO comparison, and Eydna's three-listing queen catalogue holds the same core shape, just with a sharper price-versus-feature contradiction sitting in the middle of it.

What genuinely matches across all three Eydna listings: every one finishes at the standard 84×90″ queen dimension, sized to cover a queen mattress with overhang on both sides rather than a full or king variant relabeled. Every title also states dual control — the light-gray listing spells it out furthest, naming "Dual Controllers & Dual Heating Zones" directly, while the white and denim-blue titles shorten it to "Dual Control." We're treating that as the same architecture across all three rather than a lesser feature on two of them, since a dual-control queen blanket in this category is built around two independently heated halves as a matter of course — one controller per sleeper is the entire reason the feature exists. All three are also stated as machine washable.

Auto-off duration is where the match gets looser than it looks at a glance. The light-gray and white listings both state "1-10 Hours Auto Off" — a selectable window you set yourself. The denim-blue listing states "10Hs Auto Off" with no range given, which reads as a fixed ten-hour shutoff rather than something you can dial down to one or two hours for a shorter evening. All three land inside what we'd call the ten-hour range, but only two of the three let you choose where inside it you stop.

Safety language splits a similar way. "Overheat Protect" appears directly in the titles of the white and denim-blue listings. It does not appear in the light-gray listing's title at all. We're not going to assume the light-gray blanket lacks overheat protection just because the phrase is absent from its title — sellers cut words from titles for space, not necessarily because a feature is missing — but we're also not going to claim it has a protection the title doesn't state. None of the three titles mentions a named certification like ETL or UL; "Overheat Protect" and the stated auto-off windows are the closest thing to a safety claim any of them makes, and that claim itself isn't universal across all three.

The number that runs backwards

Nine levels at $49.99, five levels at $59.99

This is the detail that makes the Eydna catalogue worth a closer look rather than a shrug. The light-gray listing states nine heating levels. The white listing also states nine heating levels. The denim-blue listing — the most expensive of the three by ten dollars — states five. In a category where more heat-level granularity is almost always treated as the upsell, finding the priciest listing with the fewest settings is backwards enough to flag on its own.

More levels matter for a practical reason, not a marketing one: a blanket with only five settings has to space its "low" and "high" further apart than one with nine, which means the gap between "barely warm" and "too hot" is wider and harder to land in the middle of. Nine levels gives you finer steps to find the one that actually matches the room temperature and how cold you personally run. Whether nine is meaningfully better than, say, six or eight in practice is its own question — we worked through a similar "does more actually mean more control" argument in our piece on a queen blanket claiming twenty heat levels, and the short version is that gains past a certain point get marginal. Going from nine down to five, though, is a large enough step that it's a real reduction in how finely you can tune the blanket, not a rounding difference.

It's also worth being precise about what "9 heat levels" or "5 heat levels" does and doesn't tell you. None of the three Eydna titles states an actual temperature range in degrees for any setting — they're all abstract level counts, the way almost every blanket in this category is labeled. We covered the handful of listings that break from that convention in our guide to the one heated blanket that states its actual temperature; Eydna isn't one of them, on any of the three colors. So "nine levels" tells you how finely you can adjust, not how hot the top setting actually runs — a caveat worth keeping in mind whichever of the three you buy.

We don't have a stated reason, in any of the three listings, for why the denim-blue blanket costs more while offering fewer levels and a less flexible auto-off. We're not going to guess at Amazon catalogue pricing logic on the seller's behalf. We can say plainly that if heat-level granularity and a selectable auto-off matter to you, paying the $59.99 premium for the denim-blue listing buys you less of both, not more.

The two cheaper listings

Microplush gray versus double-sided sherpa white: what actually separates them

If price, heat-level count, and auto-off range are identical between the light-gray and white listings — and on paper they are, both at $49.99, both at nine levels, both on the selectable one-to-ten-hour window — then the decision between the two cheaper Eydna blankets comes down almost entirely to fabric and finish, not specification.

The light-gray listing (B0FFB37B3Y) states its fabric as "Soft Plush Microplush," a single consistent texture on both faces. The white listing (B0FFB8VXMY) states "Double-Sided Plush Sherpa," which reads as two distinct textures — a reversible blanket where each face offers something different to the touch, rather than one uniform plush surface throughout. Sherpa's looped pile also tends to run thicker and feel warmer against skin before the heating element is even switched on, which is a meaningful difference if you're choosing between the two primarily by feel rather than by color.

The white listing's title also adds "for Couch & Bed" explicitly, naming dual-room use the way the light-gray title does not. We wouldn't read that as a hard limitation on the gray blanket — a 84×90″ queen blanket works on a couch regardless of which listing you buy — but it is the kind of detail a title either spends words on or doesn't, and the white listing spent them here. Both listings use near-identical gifting language: "Gifts for Womens" on the gray title, a detail worth knowing if you're shopping with our guide to heated blankets as gifts open in another tab, since the denim-blue listing instead markets itself as "Gifts for Family" — a small but real signal about who each color is positioned for.

Strip away the marketing language and the real choice between the two $49.99 listings is: a single consistent microplush texture in light gray, or a reversible double-sided sherpa-and-plush texture in white that also calls out couch use directly. Neither buys you more heat settings or a longer auto-off than the other — that part of the decision is already settled before you look at color or fabric at all.

The question most shoppers skip

Is the denim-blue version worth paying more for a feature downgrade?

Line the three Eydna listings up and the denim-blue blanket is the only one asking you to pay a premium for less: ten dollars more than either of its siblings, four fewer heat levels, and a fixed rather than selectable auto-off window. In almost any other comparison on this site, that combination would be an easy pass. It isn't automatically a bad buy here, but it does mean the burden of proof sits entirely on whether you specifically want that color.

The honest case for the denim-blue listing is narrow. "Energy Efficient" is the one phrase its title uses that neither the gray nor the white title does, and if that claim holds up in practice — which we can't confirm one way or the other without a stated wattage figure — it's a real point in its favor for anyone specifically weighing running cost over control. "Overheat Protect" also appears here, same as it does on the white listing, so it isn't behind on every safety-adjacent claim, just the ones tied to granularity and timer flexibility. And denim blue is simply a color option the other two don't offer; if neither light gray nor white suits your bedroom, color alone can be a legitimate reason to pay more.

What it doesn't offer is any control advantage to offset the price. Five heat levels is a real step down from nine, not a marginal one, and a fixed ten-hour auto-off removes the ability to set a shorter window for an evening nap or a quick pre-warm before bed. If you're choosing purely on what the blanket lets you do once it's plugged in, the math doesn't favor denim blue at any price, let alone a higher one. Our general rule for this kind of same-brand spread, which held up the same way across the ESTINGO queen blankets and Eastsora's two nearly-identical heated throws: read the full title of the exact listing you're about to buy, treat anything it doesn't state as unconfirmed, and don't assume the pricier option in a brand's lineup is automatically the better-specified one. Here, it plainly isn't.

FAQ

Common questions

Why does the cheaper Eydna blanket have more heat levels than the pricier one?

We don't have a stated reason from Eydna for this. The $49.99 light-gray and white queen listings both state nine heating levels with a selectable one-to-ten-hour auto-off. The $59.99 denim-blue listing states five heating levels and a fixed ten-hour auto-off. Paying more buys you a different color and a stated "Energy Efficient" claim, not more control — that part runs in the opposite direction from what the price would suggest.

What's the real difference between the light-gray and white Eydna queen blankets?

On control specs, nothing: both are $49.99, both list nine heat levels, and both offer the same selectable one-to-ten-hour auto-off. The difference is fabric and framing. The light-gray listing states a single "Soft Plush Microplush" texture; the white listing states "Double-Sided Plush Sherpa," a reversible two-texture fabric, and its title also explicitly mentions use on a couch as well as a bed, and states overheat protection directly where the gray listing's title does not.

Do all three Eydna queen blankets have overheat protection?

We can only confirm it where it's stated. "Overheat Protect" appears directly in the titles of the white and denim-blue listings. It does not appear in the light-gray listing's title. We're not assuming it's absent from the gray blanket just because the title doesn't mention it, but we're also not claiming it's present without that confirmation — if that specific detail matters to you, check it directly on the listing before buying.

Should I buy the denim-blue Eydna blanket?

Only if denim blue is specifically the color you want, or the stated "Energy Efficient" claim matters more to you than heat-level granularity. At $59.99 it costs ten dollars more than either the light-gray or white listing while offering five heat levels instead of nine and a fixed rather than selectable auto-off. On control alone, the two cheaper listings are the better buy.
