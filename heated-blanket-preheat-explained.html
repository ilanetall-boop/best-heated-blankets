What the Preheat Function on a Heated Blanket Actually Does

Run hot for a short window, then drop automatically to a lower level and hold it there for hours. That is the entire feature. It sounds small until you notice it is doing a job you used to do yourself, by hand, usually without thinking about it — and that a surprising number of blankets still make you do manually.

By Ilane Tall · Updated October 27, 2026 · Research-based.
Quick answer

Hot first, then a long, lower hold

Preheat runs a heated blanket at a high setting for a short, fixed window right after you turn it on, then switches automatically to a lower setting and keeps it there for the rest of the session. It exists to remove one specific step: manually dialing the blanket down once it has actually warmed up, which most people either forget to do or do too late. The blankets we cover below all build this in; plenty of cheaper ones still leave the whole job to you.

The problem it solves

The step preheat removes

Without preheat, warming up a heated blanket is a two-stage job you have to manage yourself. You turn the dial to a high setting because the blanket is cold and so are you, wait for it to actually feel warm, and then — this is the part people skip — turn the setting back down, because the level that felt right when you were cold is too much once you have warmed up. Leave it on high and you end up pushing the blanket off, sweating under it, or reaching over in the dark to knock the dial down.

Most people do not perform that second step reliably. They fall asleep before they get to it, get distracted, or never learn the dial is supposed to move twice rather than once. The result is a blanket that runs too hot for most of the session, or one that gets switched off entirely out of self-defense — which defeats the point of owning it.

A preheat function automates exactly that second step. You still choose to turn the blanket on, but you no longer have to notice when it has warmed up enough to need turning down, or be awake to do it. The blanket runs its own short hot phase, then drops itself to the level you would have dialed to manually if you had remembered. It is a small piece of automation aimed at the one habit most people never form on their own.

Mechanics

What the cycle looks like: hot, then hold

On the throws we cover here, preheat is marketed as fast heating: the blanket runs at a high setting for a short period right after you switch it on, so the fabric and the air trapped against your body reach a comfortable temperature quickly rather than creeping up gradually. The Bedsure Heated Blanket Throw with Preheat Function names this directly — fast heating is the preheat phase, the reason the blanket feels warm within minutes rather than a slow ramp.

Once that initial window ends, the blanket drops to a lower, sustained setting on its own, and stays there for the rest of whatever timer duration you selected. You do not reach over and turn anything down; the handoff from hot to hold happens inside the controller's own logic. On a throw with six heat settings and six timer presets to ten hours, the high phase is brief and the hold phase is the overwhelming majority of the session — the entire design intent. The feature is not about running hot longer; it is about running hot for as short a time as possible before settling into the level you actually want to live with.

The clearest published version of this cycle among our picks is the Bedsure Ribbed Electric Blanket, Twin, where preheat is described as matched to an eight-hour sleep cycle: roughly two hours at heat level six, then six hours at level four. That is a textbook hot-then-hold shape, scaled to how long a person is actually asleep rather than to a round number — and it says something preheat marketing rarely states outright: the hold phase runs several times longer than the hot phase, not a rough split down the middle.

Choosing the right hold

Matching the hold to how long you are actually there

Preheat decides how the blanket warms up; the timer decides how long it keeps running at all, and the two settings need to agree with how long you are genuinely going to be under the blanket. The throws here offer six timer presets to ten hours, and the twin blanket offers the same range built around its eight-hour cycle. Picking the wrong one does not break anything, but it either cuts the session short while you are still using the blanket, or leaves it running long after you have gotten up and left it on the sofa.

For an evening on the sofa, a shorter preset — something in the one-to-three-hour range — usually matches the actual block of time better than defaulting to the longest option available. For an overnight bed session, the longer end of the range is worth reading alongside the specific blanket's auto-off behaviour, because a shut-off set too short leaves you cold at 3 a.m. and one set too long may run well past when you wanted heat. We cover that shut-off mechanics, including why some blankets cut out after two hours and others run to twelve, in our separate guide to heated blanket auto-off timers.

The hold level is the other half of the match. A hold near the top of the range suits someone who runs cold or keeps a bedroom genuinely chilly; a hold near the middle or bottom of the six available levels suits most people once they have actually warmed up, which is the whole premise behind the lower half of the cycle. There is no universal right answer — it depends on your room, your tolerance for heat, and whether you share the blanket — but the preset timer and the hold level are the two numbers worth adjusting together rather than leaving both at the factory default.

Where you use it

Preheat on a sofa versus preheat on a bed

On a sofa, preheat is almost entirely about the first few minutes. You sit down, you are cold, and you want the blanket to feel warm before the show you are watching gets through its opening credits. The fast-heating phase does real work here, and because a sofa session is short and actively supervised — you are awake and able to reach the controller — the hold phase that follows matters less precisely. If the hold level ends up slightly off, you notice immediately and can adjust it yourself, a safety net you do not have while asleep.

On a bed, the priorities flip. The preheat phase is there to make the bed itself warm before you get in, a different job from warming a person already sitting under the blanket. The twin blanket's stated two-hour-at-level-six phase, timed ahead of an eight-hour sleep, is built around that exact use case: switch it on before you finish your evening routine, and by the time you climb in, the bed has already done its hot phase and is moving into, or already sitting in, the long hold. Unlike the sofa, you are not awake to correct a hold level that turns out wrong, which is why the hold half of a bed-oriented cycle is deliberately set lower and longer rather than hot and short.

This is also where blanket size and bed size start to matter more than preheat itself. A twin-sized electric blanket is built to cover a twin mattress specifically, a different proposition from a 50×60-inch throw spread over a larger bed and leaving cold edges exposed. Doing it manually on a blanket without the feature

Plenty of heated blankets, including older models and budget ones, still leave the hot-then-hold handoff entirely to you. You can approximate the same result by hand: start on the highest heat setting the blanket offers, let it run for roughly ten to twenty minutes while the fabric and trapped air come up to temperature, then turn the dial down yourself to whatever level you intend to hold for the rest of the session. The only skill involved is remembering to do the second half.

For a sofa session, switch the blanket on shortly before you sit down — while making tea or finishing the dishes — so the hot phase has already happened, then dial down almost as soon as it feels properly warm rather than waiting until it feels too warm. Because you are awake for the whole session, a missed step is a minor annoyance you can correct on the spot.

For a bed, the stakes differ, because you will likely be asleep before any forgotten adjustment gets corrected. Turn the blanket on at a high setting before your evening routine so it has a head start warming the bed, then deliberately drop it to a lower, conservative setting before you get in — treat that as a fixed habit rather than something to remember once you are already drowsy. Pair it with checking the blanket's actual auto-off duration in our auto-off timer guide, since a manual routine does not change how long the blanket runs unattended. Either way, never fold or tightly tuck a blanket while it is running, and before washing it, see our guide on how to wash a heated blanket without ruining it for the one detail — a detachable controller — that decides whether that is even possible.

FAQ

Common questions

Does preheat use more electricity than just leaving a blanket on one setting?

Not meaningfully. The preheat phase runs at a high setting for a short, fixed window — minutes, not hours — and the much longer hold phase that follows runs at a lower level. Across a full session, most of your runtime is spent at that lower hold setting, not the brief hot phase, so the overall energy use is close to what a blanket run at a moderate setting the whole time would draw. Our separate breakdown of what a heated blanket actually costs to run walks through the full cents-per-evening math with its assumptions stated.

Can I just leave the blanket on a low setting the whole time instead of using preheat?

You can, and it works, but it changes how long you wait to feel warm. A low, steady setting avoids any hot phase at all, which means it takes longer for the blanket to feel noticeably warm than it would with a dedicated preheat or fast-heating phase doing that work up front. Preheat exists specifically to shorten that wait without requiring you to start on a high setting and remember to turn it down yourself.

How long does the hot phase of preheat actually last?

It varies by model and is usually short relative to the full session. The clearest published example among our picks is the twin blanket, whose preheat is described as about two hours at heat level six within an eight-hour cycle, before dropping to level four for the remaining six hours. Throws marketed on fast heating describe a quicker initial warm-up rather than a multi-hour phase, since a sofa session is typically much shorter than a night's sleep.

Does preheat matter if I only use my blanket on the sofa for an hour?

It matters more for how quickly the blanket feels warm than for anything else, since a short sofa session does not give a slow-ramping blanket much time to reach a comfortable temperature before you get up again. A preheat or fast-heating phase gets you to that comfortable point within minutes rather than gradually over most of the session, which is specifically useful for shorter, supervised sessions where you want the payoff immediately rather than eventually.
