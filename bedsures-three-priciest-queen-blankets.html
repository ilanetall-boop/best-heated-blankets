Bedsure's Three Priciest Queen Blankets: What $85 to $95 Buys

Bedsure's entry-level queen electric blanket lists for under $60. Above that sit three listings that cost noticeably more — a plain grey, a box-quilted ivory and a thick striped flannel — and the gap between them is not just color. One of the three is built differently from the other two in a way that has nothing to do with styling.

By Ilane Tall · Updated November 27, 2026 · Research-based.
Quick answer

Same safety claims, same dual control — different heat levels and a different build

All three of Bedsure's priciest queen listings — the plain grey Dual Control Electric Blanket ($72.65), the Luxury Quilted ivory ($84.78) and the Thick Striped Flannel ($85.48) — carry dual control and the same stated Intertek-recognized safety testing. That is where the similarity ends. The grey and the striped flannel both top out at six heat settings; the quilted ivory climbs to ten and is the only one of the three with box quilting and down-alternative fill between the layers, which is a physical construction difference, not a different print on the same blanket. The odd detail worth knowing before you compare prices head-on: the striped flannel is the most expensive of the three, yet it has fewer heat settings and a shorter, fixed auto-off window than the quilted ivory beneath it in price.

This guide works through what stays constant across all three, the heat-level and timer gap, the one listing that is physically different rather than just restyled, our picks among the three, and whether the money between them buys anything beyond fill and heat levels. The baseline

What doesn't change no matter which one you buy

Move up from Bedsure's entry queen blanket to any of these three pricier listings and two things stay fixed regardless of which one you pick. The first is dual control: every one of the three is built so each side of the bed gets its own controller and its own setting, which is the baseline feature that defines a "queen electric blanket" in Bedsure's catalog rather than an upgrade you pay extra for. The grey model names it directly in its title — Dual Control Electric Blanket — and both the quilted ivory and the striped flannel repeat the same spec. If you are comparing Bedsure queen listings purely on dual control, there is nothing to decide here; it is standard across the price range once you are above the cheapest single-control options.

The second constant is the safety claim. Bedsure states its blankets are evaluated in an Intertek-recognized testing program, a specific, checkable claim rather than a vague "certified safe" badge, and it applies to this trio the same way it applies to the rest of the brand's queen and throw lineup. None of the three listings here publish a different certification tier or a lab name of their own — they all lean on the same brand-wide program. That consistency is worth knowing before you shop on safety marks alone: paying more within this trio does not buy a higher safety tier, because there isn't one to buy. If automatic shut-off and certification are what you're actually shopping for, how a blanket's control count relates to the safety features it ships with is worth reading before you assume more dials means more protection.

Preheat is the third shared feature, and it is easy to miss because it doesn't show up as a line item you compare in a spec table — it's a behavior built into how the controller ramps the blanket up. Rather than starting cold and asking you to dial through settings until it feels right, each of these runs a brief high-heat phase first, then settles to whatever level you've actually chosen. It's the same logic Bedsure uses across its throw lineup, and it's one of the few things in this comparison that genuinely doesn't change as the price climbs from $72.65 to $85.48.

Control

Six heat levels on two of the three, ten on the quilted model

Here is where the three listings actually separate. The striped flannel publishes the most complete spec sheet of the group: six heat settings and six time settings, with an 8-hour timer auto shut-off. That gives you six fixed points to choose a warmth level, paired with six preset run-time windows that cap out at eight hours — enough for a full night's sleep, though not adjustable beyond that ceiling. The quilted ivory doubles the heat-setting count to ten, and instead of fixed timer presets it offers a selectable auto-off anywhere from one to ten hours, so the same blanket that gives a finer gradient of warmth also gives you more control over exactly how long it stays on. Bedsure markets this model's fast-heating behavior alongside the preheat function, which is consistent with the extra settings being a genuine control upgrade rather than a marketing number.

The plain grey sits at six heat settings too, the same count as the striped flannel, which is the detail that makes the pricing gap between these two listings hard to justify on control alone — they offer the same number of heat levels, yet the striped flannel currently lists about thirteen dollars higher. What the grey listing does not publish, and what we are not going to guess at, is a specific auto-off window; the listing simply doesn't break that figure out the way the other two do. That gap in published detail is itself worth noting, because it means buyers comparing all three side by side are working with uneven information — two listings that spell out exact hour ranges, and one that doesn't, independent of what any of them actually cost.

If the number of heat settings is the thing you're actually optimizing for when you shop queen blankets, it's worth knowing this isn't unique to Bedsure's catalog — plenty of brands price their highest heat-level count below a model with fewer settings, for reasons that have nothing to do with control. We've seen the identical pattern play out with another brand's three queen blankets, where the two cheapest carried more heat levels than the priciest, and separately asked whether a blanket advertising twenty heat levels actually buys more usable control than one with half that count. The short version in both cases, and in this one: heat-setting count tracks construction and features more than it tracks price.

Construction

The box-quilted ivory is the one that's physically different

It would be easy to assume these three listings are the same blanket in three colorways, the way a lot of electric-blanket catalogs work — same heating grid, same fabric, different swatch photo. That assumption holds for two of the three here and breaks for the third. The striped flannel is exactly what its name says: a flat, thick flannel panel in a white-and-grey stripe, with the heating wire run through a single layer of fabric the way most electric blankets are built. The plain grey listing doesn't name a specific fabric in its title at all, which again points to it being the least-documented of the three, but nothing in what Bedsure does publish suggests it departs from that same flat-panel construction.

The quilted ivory does depart from it. Bedsure builds this one as a box-quilted 84x90 piece with down-alternative fill sitting between the quilted layers, marketed explicitly as "comforter-like warmth" rather than throw-like warmth. That is a different manufacturing process from a flat flannel or microplush panel: box quilting stitches the fabric into a grid of individual pockets, and each pocket holds fill the way a comforter or a duvet insert does, so the blanket has loft and weight to it even before the heating element does anything. A flat panel has no fill to trap air independently of the wire; a quilted, filled piece does. That is the one place in this trio where paying more buys a genuinely different object, not just a different name on the box.

This is the same distinction worth checking on any electric blanket marketed as "luxury" or "plush" — the word alone doesn't tell you whether there's fill behind it or just a heavier-weight single fabric. We've looked at the same question from a different angle in a comparison of heated throws with a textured surface, where jacquard, waffle weave and faux fur all changed the feel of the blanket without necessarily adding fill the way box quilting does. The quilting-and-fill distinction here is the queen-size version of that same question: does the extra money buy a different texture, or a genuinely different internal structure? For the ivory listing, it's the latter.

The honest take

What the extra money buys — and what it doesn't

Line the three up by price and the story is not a clean staircase where each dollar buys a little more. From the grey at $72.65 to the quilted ivory at $84.78 — a jump of roughly twelve dollars — you get four additional heat settings, a selectable auto-off window instead of none published, and an entirely different internal construction with box quilting and down-alternative fill. That is a jump that buys something concrete and checkable at every step. It is the kind of upgrade worth paying for if a queen blanket is going to be your main bedding through a full winter rather than a backup.

Keep going to the striped flannel at $85.48 — another eighty cents past the quilted ivory — and the value curve flattens, then reverses. You are not buying more heat settings; you're buying four fewer. You're not buying a selectable timer; you're buying a fixed eight-hour cap. You're not buying fill; you're buying a flat flannel panel. The only thing that eighty-cent premium over the quilted ivory buys is the specific look: a striped pattern in white rather than a solid ivory with box quilting. If you want that look, buying it is a legitimate choice — you know exactly what you're trading away. But measured purely on function, the most expensive listing in this trio is not the best-equipped one, and anyone shopping by price-equals-quality would walk right past the better-specified blanket sitting underneath it.

That pattern — a mid-priced listing out-featuring the top-priced one from the same brand — is common enough in this category that it's worth checking every time rather than assuming price order tracks feature order. We found the same thing when we looked at four queen listings from a different brand priced within six dollars of each other, where color and one cord-length figure were nearly the only things that changed. The lesson carries over directly here: read the heat-setting count and the auto-off window on the actual listing before you buy, and don't assume the highest number on the price tag means the highest number anywhere else on the spec sheet.

FAQ

Common questions

Are all three of these Bedsure queen blankets the same size?

The quilted ivory and the striped flannel are both explicitly listed at 84x90 inches, standard queen dimensions. The plain grey listing is sold as "Queen" without a stated inch measurement in its title, so while it's reasonable to expect it falls in the same general range, we can't confirm an exact figure from what Bedsure publishes for that specific listing — check the current listing's size chart before buying if the exact dimension matters to you.

Does the box-quilted ivory actually have fill inside it, or is it just a thicker outer shell?

Bedsure markets this one with down-alternative fill between box-quilted layers, built to feel like a comforter rather than a flat heated panel. That's a different manufacturing approach from the striped flannel and the plain grey, both of which are flat, single-layer constructions with the heating wire run directly through the fabric and no separate fill layer.

Why does the striped flannel cost more than the quilted model with fewer heat settings?

At current prices it does — $85.48 against $84.78 — and the gap doesn't track features. The quilted ivory has ten heat settings and a selectable one-to-ten-hour auto-off; the striped flannel has six heat settings and a fixed eight-hour cap. The extra money on the flannel buys its specific pattern and white colorway, not more control or a different internal construction. Prices on both move regularly, so confirm current figures before assuming the gap still runs this direction.

Which of these three should I actually buy?

If you want the best-equipped blanket for the money, the quilted ivory at $84.78 wins on heat settings, timer flexibility and construction. If you want queen coverage and dual control at the lowest price in this trio, the plain grey at $72.65 does that without the quilted premium. The striped flannel is worth its price only if the specific striped, white look is what you're shopping for — on every other measure it's outperformed by the model priced just below it.
