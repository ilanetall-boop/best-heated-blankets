The budget floor

What falls away first at this price

Thirty dollars is roughly where the heated-blanket category stops being dominated by names you'd recognize and starts filling up with brands that exist almost entirely inside this one price band. That shift changes what a listing tells you before it changes anything about how the blanket actually performs. The first thing to disappear is detail: a heat-level count stated as a number, an auto-off duration given in hours, a fabric weight in grams per square meter, a named testing programme. None of these cost the manufacturer anything to publish — they're just words on a listing — and yet under $30 they're the first casualty, not the heating wire or the stitching.

Compare the three products in this guide and the pattern is visible in the data we actually have. The Reaks throw, at $28.49, still states five heat levels and a three-hour auto-off plainly in its title and listing. The Hansleep throw, one dollar more at $29.99, describes itself as a "soft electric blanket" at the same 50×60″ size and color choice of grey, but its listing doesn't give a heat-level count or a timer duration the way Reaks's does. Neither omission necessarily means the feature is worse or missing entirely — automatic shut-off is standard across US-market electric blankets as a safety baseline, price aside — it means the number itself isn't something we can verify and therefore isn't something we'll assert on your behalf.

The second thing to go is brand recognition. A $70 queen blanket is more likely to carry a name you've seen in a department store; a $28 throw is more likely to carry a name that exists only as a listing. That isn't automatically a problem — Reaks and Hansleep both publish real, checkable dimensions and a real price — but it does mean you're trusting a specification sheet rather than a brand's broader reputation, which is exactly why reading the sheet matters more here than anywhere else in this category.

The checklist

The specifications still worth demanding under $30

Even at the bottom of the category, a handful of specifics are reasonable to insist on before you click buy, because at least one $28-to-$30 throw in this guide manages to publish all of them. The first is finished dimensions stated in inches, not a vague "throw size" — both Reaks and Hansleep give you 50×60″ plainly, which is the standard one-person throw footprint used across this entire category.

The second is a heat-level count given as a number. Reaks states five heat levels, which is enough room to find something between "barely on" and "too much" without needing six or nine settings the way pricier blankets advertise. The third is an auto-off duration in hours rather than just the word "automatic." Reaks states three hours, which is honest information even though it's shorter than the ten-to-twelve-hour ranges you'll find on throws that cost $10 or $15 more — a three-hour cutoff suits an evening on the sofa, and knowing the number lets you decide for yourself whether that's enough, rather than discovering it the hard way partway through a movie.

The fourth is a named safety-testing claim, which is harder to find under $30 than any other spec on this list. Of the three throws here, only the Beautyrest, at $36.84, states a specific testing claim — its listing says each unit passes seventy-eight independent safety tests. Reaks and Hansleep don't make an equivalent claim in their listings, which doesn't mean they lack any safety certification, only that we have nothing specific to cite for either one. If a named testing programme matters to you more than anything else on this list, it's the clearest reason to look at the $36.84 pick rather than the two under $30. Before you plug anything in

Fabric weight as the honest proxy for warmth

A heated throw is warmed by two things working together: the wire running through it, and the fabric itself trapping your own body heat. The second half of that equation is where fabric weight becomes the most useful, least marketing-dependent number in the entire category — a heavier pile holds warmth on its own, before the element does any work, while a thin, flat fabric relies almost entirely on the wire to feel warm at all.

The trouble is that fabric weight, stated as grams per square meter, is itself a specification that tends to disappear under $30. Reaks describes its throw in general terms without a gsm figure, and Hansleep's listing does the same. Neither statement is dishonest — "soft" and "throw blanket" are accurate descriptions — but neither gives you the one number that would let you compare their fabric to anything else objectively. The Beautyrest Reversible, again, is the exception: its listing states 200 gsm plush on one face and 220 gsm sherpa on the other, which is heavier than the thin fleece construction common at the very bottom of this category and is also the single most specific fabric claim among the three products in this guide.

What this means practically is that under $30, fabric weight is a proxy you'd like to use and frequently can't, because the number isn't published. In its absence, the next-best honest signal is the heat-level count and auto-off duration Reaks does give you, since a blanket that bothers to state those numbers is at least giving you something concrete to judge it by. All three throws here are stated as machine washable once the controller is detached — see our full guide on how to wash a heated blanket without ruining it for the detail that actually matters before that first cycle.

The next price tier

When spending $15 more changes the experience

Inside this guide, the jump from Reaks or Hansleep to the Beautyrest is only $7 to $8, and it already buys a named fabric weight and a stated safety-testing claim. Keep climbing by another $10 to $15 past that — into the roughly $40-to-$45 band where the wider throw market sits, which we cover in full in our guide to the best heated throw blankets — and what changes is less about the blanket getting warmer and more about the listing getting more specific everywhere at once: a named preheat routine that steps the temperature down automatically after an initial warm-up, timer presets broken into named hour increments instead of a single stated number, and testing claims that name the laboratory or standard behind them rather than just a test count.

What doesn't change nearly as much as the price suggests is the running cost. A throw drawing roughly 100 watts on a mid setting for eight hours costs on the order of 14 cents in electricity at a typical US residential rate, regardless of whether you paid $28 or $45 for the blanket itself — see the full arithmetic, with its assumptions stated in the open, in our guide on what a heated blanket actually costs to run. The extra money at the next price tier buys published detail and, in some cases, a heavier or more specific fabric; it does not meaningfully change what the blanket costs to leave running most evenings through winter.

The other place the extra spend matters is bed coverage rather than throw comfort. None of the three picks in this guide are sized for a bed, and if the problem you're actually solving is a queen or king mattress rather than one person on a sofa, the relevant next step isn't a pricier throw at all — it's a full-size or queen electric blanket, which we cover in our guide to the best heated blankets for a queen bed, or a model built for two independent sleepers in our dual-control roundup. Spending more on a throw doesn't solve a bed-sized problem; buying the right category does.

If you're new to the category

The buy order: cheap throw first if you have never owned one

If you've never owned a heated throw and aren't sure whether you'll reach for one most evenings or forget about it after the first cold week, the sensible order is to start at the bottom of this guide's price range, not the top. Reaks or Hansleep, at $28.49 and $29.99, let you test the entire premise — whether a plugged-in blanket actually changes how you feel sitting still in a cold room — for less than the cost of a single takeout dinner for two. If the answer is yes, you've lost almost nothing by starting cheap; if the answer is no, you haven't spent $70 finding that out on a queen blanket you'll barely use.

Only after that first season is it worth moving up the price ladder deliberately, and the direction you move should follow the specific thing the cheap throw didn't give you, not price for its own sake. If what bothered you was the three-hour auto-off cutting out mid-evening, the fix is a longer stated timer, which is exactly what the step up to roughly $40 buys in our heated throw blankets guide. If what you actually wanted was to warm a shared bed rather than a reading chair, no throw at any price solves that — that's a different product category, covered in our queen-bed roundup and, if a single controller becomes an argument, our dual-control guide. And if your actual complaint was a cold mattress rather than a cold person, the right next purchase isn't a blanket at all; our comparison of heated throws against heated mattress pads walks through that fork directly.

The one thing not to do is skip the cheap throw entirely because it feels like a compromise. A $28 throw with a stated heat-level count and auto-off, like Reaks, is a complete, usable product on its own terms — it's a starting point precisely because it's cheap enough to be wrong about, not because it's a lesser version of something else.

FAQ

Common questions

What's the cheapest heated blanket worth buying?

Among the three throws in this guide, the Reaks Heated Throw Blanket at $28.49 is the lowest-priced one that still publishes a complete, specific spec sheet: five heat levels and a three-hour auto-off on a standard 50×60″ throw. Price alone isn't the deciding factor at this end of the category — whether the listing states a heat-level count and a timer duration in hours is at least as important as the number on the price tag.

Do budget heated blankets have an automatic shut-off?

Automatic shut-off is standard across US-market electric blankets regardless of price, including the three throws in this guide. What varies under $30 is whether the listing states the duration in hours. Reaks states three hours plainly; Beautyrest describes automatic shut-off without naming the duration; Hansleep's listing doesn't break the timer out at all. The feature itself is present on all of them; the published detail about it is not equally complete.

Why don't some listings state the heat settings or auto-off time?

Under $30, publishing detailed specifications costs a brand nothing in manufacturing but does cost effort in writing and verifying the listing, and it's often the first thing a budget-tier brand skips. It doesn't necessarily mean the feature performs worse — the Hansleep throw in this guide is the same 50×60″ footprint and price band as Reaks, it simply doesn't name its heat-level count or timer duration the way Reaks does. Treat a missing specification as missing information, not as evidence the feature is absent or inferior.

Is it worth spending more than $30 on a heated blanket?

It depends what you're missing at the lower price. The Beautyrest Reversible, at $36.84, adds a named fabric weight and a stated safety-testing claim for about $7 to $8 more than Reaks or Hansleep, which is a meaningful jump in published detail for a small jump in price. Climbing further, toward the roughly $40-to-$45 throws covered in our wider heated throw blankets guide, buys named preheat routines and more detailed timer presets. What spending more does not meaningfully change is the running cost, which stays in the range of a few cents per evening regardless of price.
