The measurement

Full size on paper, and full size on a mattress

A standard full (also called double) mattress measures 54×75″. All three heated blankets in this guide are published at 72×84″, which is the conventional "full size" cut for electric blankets — a dimension that's always been larger than the mattress itself, because a blanket has to drape over the sides and foot of the bed rather than sit flush with the edges the way a fitted sheet does. Do the subtraction and you get roughly 9″ of overhang on each side and the foot: comfortable tuck-in room, not an enormous amount of slack.

That matters because full is the tightest-margin size in this category. A queen blanket at 90×84″ over a 60×80″ queen mattress has 15″ of overhang per side; a full blanket at 72×84″ over a 54×75″ full mattress has 9″. Less slack means less forgiveness if the blanket shifts in the night, and it's also why a full blanket run on a mattress with a thick topper or a deep pillow-top can end up looking tighter than the same model would on a plain innerspring full. None of that changes the published dimension, but it's worth picturing on your own mattress before you buy, rather than assuming "full" automatically means generous coverage the way "queen" or "king" does.

All three products here state the same 72×84″ full-size cut, so the sizing comparison in this guide is a wash — the differences that actually separate them are heat-level count, timer behaviour, and fabric, which is what the rest of this guide works through.

The control

Why heat-level counts matter more on a larger blanket

A heating wire covers more fabric on a 72×84″ full blanket than it does on a 50×60″ throw, which means the same single step between, say, level 3 and level 4 moves a larger area of you from one temperature to the next. On a small throw, a coarse jump between settings is a minor annoyance — you adjust once and move on. On a full-bed blanket you're lying under for eight hours, that same coarse jump is the difference between comfortable and sweating through the night, and you don't get to simply pull an edge off to cool down the way you can with a throw on a sofa, because the whole bed is covered.

This is exactly where the three picks in this guide separate from each other. The YUSTAL and Wapaneus blankets both publish ten heat settings, which gives you fine steps to work with across a bed-sized surface — useful for finding the one setting that's warm but not stifling for a specific room temperature and a specific blanket next to you. The Bedsure Ribbed blanket's listing doesn't publish an equivalent discrete heat-level count the way the other two do; it's built instead around a preheat-then-sustain cycle, which is a different way of solving the same problem — automating the step-down instead of handing you ten manual choices. Neither approach is wrong, but they ask different things of you: more settings means more control if you're willing to use it, and a preheat cycle means less to think about once you've set it running.

The practical takeaway: on a full-size blanket, treat the heat-level count (or the preheat logic, if that's what's offered instead) as a first-tier spec, not an afterthought. It matters more here than it would on a throw you can simply fold back if it runs hot.

The wiring

Even heating across a wider surface

A heating element has to run through a larger area of fabric on a full-size blanket than on a throw, and how that wire is distributed is what decides whether the whole bed warms at roughly the same rate or whether one spot runs hotter than another. The Bedsure Ribbed blanket's construction uses a ribbed-knit fabric, where the fabric itself is built with channels running through it — a structure that gives the heating wire a defined path to sit in rather than floating loosely inside a flat weave, which as a general textile matter can translate into steadier contact between the element and the surrounding fabric across a wider span. The YUSTAL and Wapaneus blankets are both described as soft flannel, a simpler flat-weave construction that's common at this size and price point; neither listing breaks out a specific wire-distribution claim beyond the stated heat-setting count.

What none of the three listings publish is an independent measurement of edge-to-edge temperature variance — that's not a spec Amazon listings in this category typically carry, and we won't estimate a number we don't have. What you can compare honestly is what's actually published: a named ribbed construction with an automated preheat cycle at the top of the price range, and two flannel blankets with a higher discrete heat-setting count in the lower-to-middle range. If even heating across the full width of the bed is the thing you care about most and you have no way to verify it directly, the practical move is to buy from the two picks with overheat protection explicitly stated on the listing — both YUSTAL and Wapaneus name it — since that's the one related safety mechanism that's actually confirmed in writing for this guide's picks.

Living with it

Weight, and what it does to a made bed

None of the three listings in this guide publish a fabric weight in grams per square meter, which is the number that would let you compare bulk directly, so we won't invent one. What you can reason about without that figure is the layering problem a full-size electric blanket creates regardless of brand: at 72×84″, it's a bed-sized layer that goes somewhere in your existing bedding stack, and where you put it changes both how it performs and how the bed looks once it's made. Put it directly against the sheet and under the comforter, and it heats efficiently but adds visible bulk under a fitted top sheet that can make hospital-cornered edges harder to keep crisp. Put it over the comforter as the top layer, and it's easier to smooth flat for a made-bed look, but you lose some of the heat to the open air above rather than trapping it against you.

The flannel construction on the YUSTAL and Wapaneus blankets tends to pack flatter and add less visible bulk under a comforter than a heavier knit would, which is worth factoring in if a slim, unobtrusive profile under your existing bedding matters to you. The ribbed-knit Bedsure blanket, by the nature of its channeled construction, sits with slightly more texture and loft. Either way, the honest framing is that a full-size electric blanket is one more layer to manage when you make the bed each morning, not a swap-in replacement for a blanket you already own — budget a few extra seconds for smoothing it flat, and plan on which side of the comforter stack it goes before the first cold night, not after.

The other option

When to step up to queen instead

All three picks in this guide are sized for a full mattress, and for some buyers full is the wrong size to buy at all. If two adults regularly share the bed, a full mattress at 54×75″ is already tight for two people before a blanket enters the picture, and a 72×84″ blanket over it gives each side less than its own full-width portion once you account for the overlap down the middle. A queen blanket at roughly 90×84″, covered in our separate guide to the best dual-control heated blankets, is built for exactly that situation, with some models splitting into two independently controlled zones so a shared bed doesn't mean a shared thermostat setting.

The other signal to watch for is overhang margin. We noted above that a full blanket at 72×84″ gives roughly 9″ of overhang per side on a 54×75″ mattress, compared with roughly 15″ per side for a queen blanket on a queen mattress. If your full mattress has a deep pillow-top, a thick topper, or you simply want a blanket that drapes with visible slack rather than a close, tucked fit, that extra overhang on a queen-sized blanket is the more comfortable buy even on a bed that's technically still full-size — sizing up one step is a legitimate way to trade a higher price for more forgiving coverage.

If neither of those applies — one person, a standard full mattress, no deep topper — the three picks above are sized correctly and there's no reason to pay for coverage you won't use. The decision point is genuinely that narrow: who's sleeping under it, and how much slack you want at the edges.
