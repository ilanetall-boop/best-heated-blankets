Flannel vs Sherpa vs Faux Fur: Which Heated Blanket Fabric

The heating wire is nearly the same part in every throw on the market. What actually changes how a blanket feels, dries, and holds up is the fabric wrapped around that wire — and the three main choices behave in genuinely different ways before you even plug one in. We compared three otherwise similar throws, two built from sherpa and faux fur and one from double-sided flannel, on fabric alone.

By Ilane Tall · Updated October 25, 2026 · Research-based.
Quick answer

The fabric decides warmth, drying time, and lifespan — not the settings

Flannel is lighter, dries in roughly an hour, and feels only modestly warm before you turn the heat on — the Eastsora throw is the flannel example here, double-sided and 12-level. Faux fur and sherpa, combined on the Cushious and EHEYCIGA throws, are noticeably warmer unplugged because the pile traps air, but that same pile dries slower and mats faster over repeated washes. For a blanket that lives on the sofa and gets washed often, flannel is the lower-maintenance choice; for something that feels indulgently warm before the wire does any work, sherpa and faux fur win every time.

This guide works through unplugged warmth, how each fabric carries heat from the wire, drying time, what three winters of washing does to each material, our picks among the three, and which fabric suits a bed versus a sofa. Jump straight to the three picks or to bed versus sofa.

Before the wire turns on

Warmth before you plug anything in

Every heated blanket is really two warmth sources layered on top of each other: the fabric, which insulates passively the moment you pull it over you, and the heating wire, which does the active work once it's switched on. Fabric matters first because it decides your baseline — how cold the blanket feels in the two or three seconds before the element has caught up.

The Cushious throw builds its baseline from a double layer of 260gsm faux fur on one face and 200gsm sherpa on the other, a combined weight noticeably denser than a single-layer throw. Faux fur is built from a long, raised pile that traps a thick layer of still air against your skin — the same mechanism a down coat or wool sweater relies on, insulating on its own with no electricity involved. The EHEYCIGA throw pairs the same two fabrics in a lighter reversible construction, so it starts from a similar place: warm to the touch before the wire does anything.

The Eastsora throw is built differently: double-sided flannel fleece, short fuzzy pile on both faces at a combined 260gsm and 200gsm. Flannel's pile is much shorter than faux fur's, so it traps less air and feels thinner in the hand straight out of the packaging, even though the fabric weight is close to the Cushious throw's. That shorter pile is also why flannel throws are advertised as lightweight or packable — less loft means less bulk, but also less passive warmth before the heat settings do the work.

None of this means the flannel throw runs cold — all three have heating wire through the full blanket, and the auto-off timer and heat-level count matter more to warmth after ten minutes than fabric does. But judged in the first thirty seconds, before the element has caught up, faux fur and sherpa start warmer and flannel has to catch up electrically.

Once it's switched on

How each fabric carries heat from the wire

Once the element is running, the fabric's job changes from insulating you to distributing the heat the wire generates. A thinner, denser fabric like flannel sits closer to the wire and conducts heat to your skin more directly and evenly — part of why Eastsora can offer 12 distinct heating levels: a tighter, more predictable fabric-to-skin path makes fine-grained control meaningful, because you can actually feel the difference between level 6 and level 7.

A thick pile fabric like faux fur behaves more like a buffer than a conductor. The loft that made it feel warm before you plugged it in now works against fast, even heat transfer — the wire's heat has to travel through more fiber before reaching your skin, so the blanket can feel slower to respond to a setting change. The EHEYCIGA throw's listing states it reaches a usable temperature in about ten minutes and offers nine settings across an 85–110°F range, a sensible choice for a pile fabric: fewer, more clearly spaced steps rather than fine granularity.

The Cushious throw's two faces complicate this slightly, because sherpa and faux fur aren't identical even though both count as "pile" fabrics. Sherpa is a looped knit, shorter and denser than faux fur's raised strands, so the sherpa face of a reversible blanket like this one sits a little closer to the wire's heat than the faux fur face does — a detail easy to miss on a blanket bought mainly for the look of one side.

The practical takeaway: a flannel throw rewards more heat settings because you can feel the gaps between them, while a thick pile throw rewards a longer auto-off window, since it takes longer to feel the heat arrive and longer to feel it fade once the timer cuts out.

After the wash

Drying time, and why it decides whether you own one or two

All three throws are listed as machine washable once the controller is unplugged from the blanket — standard across this category, and the one step that actually matters before you buy, covered in full in how to wash a heated blanket. What is not standard is how long each fabric takes to dry afterward, and that difference changes how you plan around laundry day.

Flannel's short, dense pile holds comparatively little water relative to its surface area, so a flannel throw like the Eastsora comes out of a wash cycle and air-dries or tumble-dries on low in roughly the time of an ordinary bedsheet — often within an hour or two. That turnaround is why a single flannel throw can realistically be a household's only one: wash it in the morning, it's back on the sofa by evening.

Faux fur and sherpa hold considerably more water in the same wash cycle, because the longer pile and looped knit both have more fiber surface area trapping moisture than a short-pile fabric does. A thick throw like the Cushious, built from 260gsm faux fur layered against 200gsm sherpa, can take most of a day to fully dry, and tumble-drying above low heat risks the heating wire woven through it — most listings, including this one, specify low heat or air-dry only. The EHEYCIGA throw, built from the same two fabrics in a lighter build, dries somewhat faster but still noticeably slower than a flannel throw of the same size.

That drying gap is the real argument for owning two heated throws rather than one. A single flannel blanket can be washed and reused the same evening. A sherpa or faux fur throw taken out of rotation for most of a day leaves nothing to reach for that night — the practical reason people who've settled on a pile fabric often end up buying a second one, not because the first wore out, but because laundry day otherwise leaves a gap.

What repeated use does

Shedding, pilling, and what three winters do

Every pile and knit fabric changes with use, and the two families here age in different directions. Faux fur is prone to shedding loose fibers in its first several washes, which is a known characteristic of synthetic long-pile fabrics generally — the fibers that were cut slightly short during manufacturing work themselves free over early wash cycles, then taper off once the pile settles. It is one reason listings in this category, including both the Cushious and EHEYCIGA throws, specify a gentle wash cycle: aggressive agitation accelerates that early shedding.

Sherpa, a tighter knit loop rather than a raised strand, sheds less than faux fur but is more prone to pilling — small fiber knots forming where loops rub against each other or against a rougher surface like a sofa arm. Over repeated winters a sherpa face can visibly mat and flatten where friction is highest, while the opposite faux fur face on a reversible throw like the Cushious tends to hold its loft a little longer, since it isn't knit into a loop structure that can unravel.

Flannel ages differently: it doesn't shed or pill the way a pile fabric does, because its fibers are shorter and brushed rather than raised or looped. What it does instead is thin — the brushed nap compresses with washing, so a flannel throw after three winters typically feels flatter and less soft than new, even though it rarely looks visibly worn the way a matted sherpa throw does.

None of this is a defect specific to any one product; it's a general property of how each fiber type is built, and it applies across the heated-blanket category broadly. The difference is cosmetic rather than functional — the heating wire's lifespan is governed by its own insulation and the certifications and safety habits you maintain, not by how the fabric around it ages.

Where it lives

The fabric that suits a bed versus a sofa

A throw's fabric matters differently depending on whether it spends its life on a sofa or under a comforter. On a sofa, a thick faux fur or sherpa throw like the Cushious or EHEYCIGA is arguably the better fit: you're sitting on top of it for a bounded stretch of time, the extra loft and the slower warm-up matter less because you're not timing it against falling asleep, and the plush feel is part of why you reach for it over a plain fleece throw in the first place. It's also easier to live with the longer drying time on a sofa blanket, since you're less likely to need it back the same evening you washed it.

Under a comforter on a bed, flannel's advantages become more relevant. A flatter, denser fabric like the Eastsora throw sits closer to the wire and responds faster to a setting change, which matters more at bedtime when you adjust the dial once and then try to fall asleep rather than fiddling with it for the next two hours. Flannel is also less bulky under a top sheet or comforter — a thick pile throw layered under bedding can trap more heat than intended once your own body heat adds to it. If you're buying for a bed shared by two people, the fabric question usually matters less than whether the blanket has independent dual-zone control, since one controller for two people is the more common complaint regardless of fabric.

Size plays into this too: all three throws here are 50×60″, sized for one person on a sofa rather than a shared bed. FAQ

Common questions

Which fabric is warmest on a heated blanket — flannel, sherpa, or faux fur?

Before you plug anything in, faux fur and sherpa feel warmer because their raised or looped pile traps a thicker layer of still air against your skin, the same principle behind a wool sweater or a down coat. Flannel's shorter, brushed pile traps less air and feels thinner by comparison straight out of the packaging. Once the heating wire is switched on, that gap narrows: a denser fabric like flannel actually conducts the wire's heat to your skin faster and more evenly than a thick pile does, so the two fabrics trade advantages depending on whether the blanket is on or off.

Does a faux fur or sherpa heated blanket take longer to dry than a flannel one?

Yes, noticeably. Faux fur's long raised pile and sherpa's looped knit both hold far more water during a wash cycle than flannel's short, dense, brushed pile, so a thick throw like the Cushious or EHEYCIGA can take most of a day to fully air-dry, while a flannel throw like the Eastsora is typically dry within an hour or two. All three are machine washable once the controller is unplugged, but check the listing for the recommended dry setting — most pile-fabric throws specify air-dry or low heat only, to protect the heating wire.

Do faux fur heated blankets shed?

Some fiber shedding in the first several washes is a known characteristic of long-pile faux fur generally, as loosely cut fibers from manufacturing work themselves free during early wash cycles before tapering off. Sherpa, being a tighter knit, sheds less but is more prone to pilling where the loops rub together or against furniture. Flannel does neither in the same way, but its brushed nap gradually compresses and thins with repeated washing instead. A gentle wash cycle, as specified on all three listings here, slows all of these effects.

Is flannel or a pile fabric better for a heated blanket on a bed?

Flannel tends to suit a bed slightly better: it's flatter and less bulky under a top sheet or comforter, it responds faster to a heat-setting change, which matters more when you adjust it once at bedtime, and it won't trap as much extra heat once your own body warmth adds to it under covers. Faux fur and sherpa throws tend to suit a sofa better, where the slower warm-up and extra loft matter less and the plush feel is part of the appeal. Either fabric works on either piece of furniture; the differences are about which trade-off you'd rather live with.
