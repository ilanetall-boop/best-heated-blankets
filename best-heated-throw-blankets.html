The size question

Why the throw outsells the bed blanket — and when it's the wrong buy

A 50×60″ throw is sized for one person: draped over your legs in a chair, pulled up to your shoulders on the sofa, or folded over the back seat of a car on a cold commute. That single-person footprint is exactly why it is the entry point for almost everyone who buys into this category — it is cheap enough to try without much risk, small enough to store in a cupboard through spring and summer, and portable in a way a queen-size electric blanket, which effectively lives on one bed, is not. All three products in this guide are throws at that same 50×60″ size, which puts them head-to-head on everything except fabric, preheat and timer.

The throw is the wrong buy in one specific situation: when the thing you are actually trying to warm is a bed shared by two people, not a person sitting still in a chair. A throw this size will not cover a queen or king mattress edge to edge, and even if it did, a single shared controller on a bed turns into the familiar argument where one person is sweating and the other is reaching for another layer. If that's your situation, a full-size or queen electric blanket — ideally one with independent dual-zone control — solves a problem a throw was never built to solve. We cover that trade-off directly in our comparison of heated throws against heated mattress pads, which gets into the same over-you-versus-under-you distinction from a different angle.

There's a second, quieter reason the throw wins by volume: it's a lower-commitment purchase for someone who genuinely doesn't know whether they'll use one past the first cold week. At $30 to $45, a throw is cheap enough to buy on a hunch; a $70-plus queen blanket is a purchase people research for weeks. If you are unsure whether a heated throw earns a permanent place in your living room, start with the throw size.

The spec nobody markets well

Preheat: what it changes about using one every evening

Preheat sounds like a minor convenience feature until you've actually lived with a throw that doesn't have it. Without preheat, you turn the throw on, sit down, and spend the first several minutes slightly cold while the fabric and the heating wire inside it catch up to whatever setting you chose. With preheat, the blanket runs at a higher setting for a short initial stretch, then automatically steps down to the level you'll actually sit under for the rest of the evening. The practical effect is that the blanket is warm by the time you've settled in, rather than warm ten minutes after you've already decided it's too cold to be worth it.

The Bedsure GentleSoft builds its whole heating routine around this idea: it runs hot briefly, then settles into a lower level for a long stretch, so you aren't manually stepping the dial down after the first ten minutes the way you would on a throw without the feature. The EHEYCIGA Faux Fur & Sherpa takes a related but different approach — it's built to reach a comfortable warmth in roughly ten minutes from a cold start, which matters less as an automated routine and more because the sherpa and faux fur construction itself is already warm before the element does any work (more on that below). The Beautyrest Reversible doesn't publish a preheat routine in its listing, which doesn't mean it heats unevenly, only that this specific behaviour isn't something we can confirm for it the way we can for the other two.

If you use a throw most evenings through winter, preheat is the kind of feature you stop consciously noticing — not because it's unimportant, but because it simply removes a step you'd otherwise perform by hand every single time. If you only reach for a throw occasionally, on the coldest nights, it matters less, because you're less likely to be impatient about the first ten minutes.

Read this before the fabric

Timer length is the spec people read last and regret first

Most people shopping for a heated throw look at the picture, then the fabric, then the price, and only skim the auto-off timer if they look at it at all. That ordering is backwards. Automatic shut-off is standard on every US-market electric throw — it's a safety feature, not an upsell — but how long it lets the blanket run before cutting out varies by model, and that single number decides whether the throw fits how you actually intend to use it.

The Bedsure GentleSoft gives you six timer presets running up to ten hours, which is long enough to cover a full evening of TV and reading without the blanket shutting off underneath you partway through. The EHEYCIGA carries a ten-hour auto-off as well, paired with nine heat settings across an 85–110°F published range, so you can dial in a specific temperature rather than guess at an abstract number. The Beautyrest Reversible states that it has automatic shut-off, which is the detail that matters for safety, though its listing doesn't break the duration down into presets the way Bedsure's does.

Here's the regret people describe after the fact: they buy a throw assuming a "heated blanket" just stays on, then find it switches off after what feels like no time at all because they never checked the number. If you want a throw running for most of a movie night, read the auto-off figure before the fabric description, not after you've clicked buy.

Before you plug anything in

Fabric weight: warm before you plug anything in

A heated throw is warmed by two things at once: the electric element woven through it, and the fabric itself trapping your own body heat. That second part is easy to overlook, but it's the reason two throws with similar heat settings can feel completely different to sit under. A heavier pile — faux fur, sherpa, thick ribbed flannel — holds warmth on its own, before the element does any work at all. A thin, flat fleece has to rely almost entirely on the wire.

Of the three throws here, the EHEYCIGA Faux Fur & Sherpa is built explicitly around this idea: faux fur on one face and sherpa on the other make it a substantial blanket before you've found the remote. The Bedsure GentleSoft uses ribbed flannel instead — lighter than a thick sherpa throw, so it dries faster after a wash, but less immediately plush out of the package. The Beautyrest Reversible publishes its fabric weight more precisely than either: 200 gsm plush on one face and 220 gsm sherpa on the other, heavier than the thin fleece most budget throws use.

The trade-off runs in the obvious direction. Heavier fabric feels warmer unplugged and holds heat longer once the element shuts off, but it also takes longer to dry after a wash and packs down less neatly into a cupboard. A lighter flannel throw is the easier thing to live with day to day; a heavier sherpa or faux-fur throw is the one that feels indulgent the moment you sit down.

The budget floor

What you give up under $30

Thirty dollars sits right at the edge of what a heated throw with a published, specific set of safety and comfort claims currently costs. The EHEYCIGA, at $29.99, is the cheapest throw in this guide and still clears that bar: nine named heat settings across a published temperature range, a ten-hour auto-off, and a fabric construction stated plainly in the listing. Go meaningfully below that price point in this category generally, and the things that tend to disappear first are exactly the specifics this guide has spent most of its time on — a named preheat routine, a published temperature or wattage range, and a specific, checkable safety-testing claim. What's left is usually a single vague heat dial and a shut-off timer that isn't broken into presets anywhere in the listing.

That's not an argument that every inexpensive throw is unsafe or won't keep you warm — automatic shut-off is standard across this category regardless of price. It's an argument for reading the listing itself rather than the price tag: if a throw doesn't tell you its heat settings, its timer range, or anything about how it's constructed, that absence is the actual trade-off you're making, not the dollar figure on the page. All three products in this guide publish that information at a price between $30 and $42, which is why we're comfortable naming them specifically rather than describing the category in the abstract.

If price is the only thing driving the decision, the EHEYCIGA is the lower-risk choice precisely because its listing is as specific as the pricier Bedsure's — you aren't giving up information for the lower number, only some timer length and the preheat routine.

FAQ

Common questions

What size is a standard heated throw blanket?

50×60 inches is the standard throw size across this category, and all three products in this guide are that size. It's built for one person — draped over your legs in a chair or pulled up to your shoulders on a sofa — rather than for covering a bed. If you need to cover a bed for one or two people, you're looking at a full-size or queen electric blanket instead, which is a larger, differently-priced product.

What does preheat actually do on a heated throw?

Preheat runs the blanket at a higher setting for a short initial stretch, then automatically steps it down to the level you'll sit under for the rest of the evening, so the throw is already warm by the time you've settled in rather than warm ten minutes later. The Bedsure GentleSoft builds its heating routine around this behaviour explicitly; the EHEYCIGA reaches a comfortable warmth in roughly ten minutes partly through its heavier fabric working alongside the element.

How long will a heated throw stay on before it shuts off?

It depends on the model, and the range varies more than most shoppers expect. The Bedsure GentleSoft offers six timer presets running up to ten hours. The EHEYCIGA carries a ten-hour auto-off as well. Automatic shut-off itself is standard on US-market throws as a safety feature; the duration before it kicks in is the number worth reading in the listing before you buy, not after.

Does a heavier fabric mean a warmer throw?

Generally, yes, and it's warm for a reason that has nothing to do with electricity: a heavier pile like faux fur or sherpa traps your own body heat on its own, before the heating element does any work. The EHEYCIGA's faux fur and sherpa construction and the Beautyrest's 200/220 gsm plush-sherpa fabric both feel warm unplugged for this reason. The trade-off is that heavier fabric takes longer to dry after a wash than a lighter ribbed flannel throw like the Bedsure's.
